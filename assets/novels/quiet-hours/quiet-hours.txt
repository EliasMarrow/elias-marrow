QUIET HOURS

A murder mystery.
Boston, 1883.


PART ONE — THE ORDER OF THINGS


Chapter One

The station house on Hanover Street kept two kinds of hours, and Detective Edward Mercer had long ago chosen his.

The loud hours ran from morning to midnight: the rail crowded, the sergeants shouting the wagon out, drunks singing in the holding cells, the whole parish of the North End passing through in its grievances and its bleeding. Useful hours. Nothing wrong with them. But nothing could be read in them, any more than a man can read a newspaper while it is being printed.

The quiet hours came after. One lamp. The stove ticking as it cooled. The day's paper settling into the books where it would keep — the blotter, the complaint book, the duty rosters, the ledger of open cases that Mercer ruled and kept himself, in a hand the whole station knew. In the quiet hours the city stopped performing and lay still on the page, and a patient man could walk through it line by line and see what it had actually done that day, as opposed to what it had said.

Mercer was fifty-two years old. He had been twenty-nine years a policeman and nineteen a detective, and in that time he had built his life the way a good clerk builds a ledger, and for the same reason. Because his wife had died of the typhoid in seventy-eight, in the sixth week of it, with the doctor still saying the word hopeful on the stairs. Because grief is a flood and a man drowning reaches for whatever holds. What had held was order. The morning walk by the same streets. The books ruled the same way. The work done in the same sequence, complaint to evidence to pattern to arrest, every case a small proof that the world answered to method — that what was broken in the night could be found, named, and set right by a man willing to be patient with paper.

He knew what the younger men said of him. That Mercer thought the Commonwealth was held up by ledgers. That he would rather read a roster than a face.

They were not wrong. Faces lied to him daily. He had never once been lied to by a roster that he did not eventually catch.

His daughter said it differently. Nell was twenty-two, and taught letters to immigrant children at the settlement school on Parmenter Street, and kept his house since her mother died, and had her mother's habit of saying the true thing while pouring the coffee, so that a man could not even leave the table in dignity.

"You believe in the books, Father," she had told him that winter, "the way Father Doyle believes in the sacraments. It's not the paper you love. It's that the paper means somebody's keeping watch."

"Somebody is," Mercer said.

"I know," she said, and poured. "I only wonder what happens to you the day the watch fails."

It was a Tuesday in January when Margaret Doran came to the rail, and the honest record of that day — and Mercer required, ever afterward, that his record of it be honest — is that he saw her, and noticed nothing, and went on up the stairs.

He was passing through the outer room with the morning's wagon-book under his arm. The rail was busy: a carter disputing a fine, an old woman about a lost dog, and at the near end a mill girl of eighteen or nineteen in a thin coat, twisting her gloves in her hands in the way of people who have rehearsed a thing all the way from home and find it dissolving on them at the door.

The officer at the complaint desk that morning was the young one, O'Rourke — patrol, lately, and marked for better; he had a quick pen and a manner. Mercer saw him lean forward on his forearms to bring his height down to the girl's, the way you might to a child at a fence, and heard him say, in a voice with all the impatience taken out of it:

"There's no hurry, miss. Nobody's turn is ahead of yours. Start where it started."

The girl's shoulders came down an inch. She began to talk. The pen began to move.

Kindness at the rail was rarer than it should have been, and Mercer noted it the way he noted any good procedure, with a small ledgered satisfaction — the system working, the frightened served, the record taken fairly — and climbed the stairs to the loud hours of his own day, and did not think of the mill girl again.

In the complaint book, in a fair round hand, the entry for that morning reads:

Jan. 11. Marg't Doran, 19, of Charter St., spinner. States a man has followed her from the mill by Prince St. three evgs. this fortnight, keeping distance, saying nothing. No description got — dusk. Advised as to route and hours. Entry taken by Ptn. J. O'Rourke.

It is all in order. It is correctly dated and legibly written and properly signed, and it sat in the book that night among forty entries like it, and the book sat on its shelf behind the rail, in the outer room, where any officer of the station might in passing turn its pages, the way a man might stand at a window looking over a field.

Nothing in the record is false.

That was the thing Mercer would say, in the end, to anyone who would listen and to several who would not: that the terrible thing was never a lie in the books.

The books were true. The books were always true.

That is what made them worth hunting from.


Chapter Two

On the last morning of her life, Margaret Doran woke before the bell and lay in the dark doing sums, which is how the poor pray.

Six dollars ten owed to Mrs. Keane for the room by Saturday. Four dollars forty in the tin. The half day Thursday if the frames ran full. It could be made to come out, if nothing happened. She said it to the ceiling the way you'd strike a bargain with it: nothing happens, and I'll make it come out.

Her sister Norah slept beside her, fifteen, new to the mill and still capable of sleeping through the first bell, which Margaret privately considered a talent that ought to be paid. She dressed without light so as not to wake her, took the heel of yesterday's loaf for her pocket, and went down the cold stairs into Charter Street, into the dark of a February morning, where her breath went ahead of her like something leading the way.

The following had stopped.

She noticed it the way you notice a toothache gone — nothing there, and then the realizing, and then the checking, gingerly, to be sure. Three weeks now. Whoever he had been — the man who kept to the far walk, half a block back, dusk after dusk, never nearer, never speaking, a shape with a hat in the failing light — he had left off. Perhaps he had frightened himself. Perhaps the man at the station had found him and said a hard word. Perhaps it had been nothing all along, a clerk who happened to walk her way, and her own fear had dressed him into a wolf.

She was ashamed of the complaint now, a little. All that fuss. The young officer had been kind about it — had told her there was no hurry, had taken it all down, had walked out to the steps with her after and pointed out the lighted way home as if he had thought about her streets before she asked. Twice since, on evenings, she had seen him on his rounds near the mill gate, and he had touched his helmet to her, and she had felt the particular safety of being known — that is the word she would have used, known — a girl the police knew by name, on streets the police walked.

She had stopped varying her route, the way he'd advised, back when there was something to vary it from. There seemed no need now. The man was gone.

The frames ran full that day and the half day Thursday was promised, and at the gate at six she reckoned that the week would come out after all, and stood a moment in the lamplight, easing her back, before starting home the near way.

Down by Prince Street the fog had come up off the harbor, and the lamps stood in it like lit wool.

She heard nothing behind her, because there was nothing behind her. That was the trick of it — the thing no one afterward would understand, because they kept imagining footsteps, pursuit, terror, a chase. There was no chase. There was a man standing at the mouth of the passage ahead of her, under the lamp, where a man may lawfully stand; and the shape of him in the fog was a shape her body had been taught for weeks to be at peace with; and when he said her name — her name, quietly, courteously, the way you speak to someone you have been asked to look after — she turned toward the sound with no more fear than she'd have turned toward a kettle.

She was not afraid. Even in the passage, even with his hands settling, there was a long moment — she had time for it; he was never hurried — a long moment in which being touched by him could still have been a mistake about to be explained.

That was the whole of what he had taught her, in three weeks of touched helmets and lighted ways.

Not to run.

The fog moved along Prince Street at its own pace, the way it had for two hundred years, and the lamps burned in it, and the bell of the Old North rang the hour into the wool and the dark.

Boston had order.

Margaret Doran had six dollars ten owed and four dollars forty in the tin, and the week was going to come out, and she had a talent-sleeping sister who would wake at dawn beside a cold place in the bed.


Chapter Three

The call came to Mercer's house before seven, and he was at Prince Street by half past.

Snow had fallen in the night, a thin dry inch of it, and the passage mouth had already been trodden to slush by half the parish before the first officer thought to hold the line, which Mercer noted sourly and said nothing about, because it was every scene, always, and a man might as well be sour at weather.

She lay ten feet in, against the brick, seated.

That was the first thing his eye filed, before name or age or the marks on her: seated. Set down against the wall with her skirts decent and her hands in her lap, in the posture of a woman resting — not the sprawl of a struggle, not the abandonment of a body dropped. Composed. Almost tended.

He crouched, and was a long time about it, and the men at the passage mouth stamped and blew and let him be, because this was Mercer, and Mercer at a scene moved at one speed and it was not theirs.

Young. Nineteen, perhaps. A spinner's hands. Her gloves were on. Her boots were dry-laced and tight — she had not been walking in fear; fear lengthens the stride and bursts the lacing; he had seen it a hundred times. No rings taken, for there were no rings; but the tin brooch at her collar untouched, and fourpence in her pocket. Not robbery.

The bruising at the throat he studied last and longest.

Two marks forward, below the jaw, thumb-breadth apart. Two behind. Narrow. Deliberate. No tearing of the collar, no scoring of the skin around, none of the frantic mauling of a strangling done in rage or panic. Pressure, laid on and held, by hands that had not needed to adjust their grip.

Dr. Hollis, when he'd done that afternoon, put it in his flat way, wiping his spectacles: "Manual compression, sustained. Two fingers and a thumb either side, and patient about it. A man in a fury crushes, Mercer. This was — " he looked for the word, and found one from his student days — "clinical."

"How long?" Mercer asked. "To do it, I mean."

"That way? Minutes. Longer than you'd think. He'd have had to hold on well past her going limp and know that going limp isn't gone." Hollis set the spectacles back. "Whoever he is, he knows how long it takes."

Mercer wrote the sentence in his book exactly as said, and boxed it, which he did with perhaps three sentences a year.

Captain Deven came down to the passage at noon with his collar up, looked what he considered long enough, and stood with Mercer in the trodden snow.

"Mill girl."

"Margaret Doran. Nineteen. Charter Street."

"Papers'll give it a day, girl like that, then let it go." Deven blew on his hands. "You'll want a man on this with you. I'm giving you O'Rourke."

"The patrol lad."

"Detective, as of the first of the month — you'd know that if you read anything but your own ledgers. He's the best of the young ones by a distance. Reads, writes a fair hand, doesn't drink to speak of, and the ward likes him." Deven paused, and gave Mercer the compliment sideways, which was the only way it was ever given in that building: "He asked for you, is the truth of it. Says there's no one else worth being taught by."

Mercer, who had refused three such assignments in five years on the grounds that a partner was a variable, looked down the passage at the brick where the girl had sat, and surprised himself.

"All right," he said.

He told himself afterward — in the honest ledger, in the quiet hours — why: because of the complaint book. He had gone back to the station that morning and turned the pages on a instinct, and found her there, eleventh of January, fair round hand. She had come to the rail. She had stood in his own outer room, frightened, and done the correct thing, the thing the whole system was for — and six weeks later she was seated against brick in Prince Street with fourpence in her pocket. The system had taken her complaint and her killer had taken her, and between those two facts there was a gap a man could fall into if he looked at it too long without working.

A young officer who leaned on his forearms to bring his height down to frightened girls; who took the entry fairly and walked them to the steps and pointed out the lighted way — that seemed to Mercer that morning, with the page open under his hand, like the part of the machine worth keeping. Worth teaching, even.

O'Rourke came up to the detectives' room that same evening, in a coat brushed better than its cloth deserved, and stood before Mercer's desk with his cap under his arm like a man reporting to a schoolmaster he'd chosen for himself.

"Mr. Mercer."

"You take the complaint," Mercer said, without greeting, and slid the book across, open at the eleventh of January. "You remember her?"

"I remember all of them," O'Rourke said, and then, catching himself, in a smaller voice: "I remember her. Doran. Followed from the mill by Prince Street. I advised her on her route." He read his own entry through, twice, and when he looked up Mercer was surprised — recorded that he was surprised — by the plain trouble in the young man's face. "I told her the lighted way, sir. She was found on it."

"She was."

"Then he knew her route better than her fear did." O'Rourke set the book down. "What do we begin with?"

And Mercer, who had waited nineteen years for a young man to ask the question in that form — not who do we grab, not what does the captain want said, but what do we begin with — pulled the lamp nearer, and gave away, in the first hour of the first evening, the whole of his method, the sentence Nell called his catechism, the sentence that was engraved above the door of everything he was:

"Begin with what answers," Mercer said. "Feelings don't answer. Faces don't answer. Paper answers. We'll start with the paper — hers, the mill's, this book's — and we'll be slower than everyone wants us to be, and we will not once guess. A guess is a debt, Mr. O'Rourke. Paper pays cash."

O'Rourke drew up the other chair and reached for his pen, and if there was anything in his face besides a student's hunger, the lamp did not show it, and Mercer did not look for it.

Why would he look?

The books were true. The rail was kind. The system had failed one girl in six weeks — failed, he'd have said then; been outrun — and here at his elbow was the fair round hand itself, asking to be taught how to hunt.

In his case ledger that night, under the boxed sentence, Mercer ruled a fresh line and entered his new partner's name, in the column he kept for resources.

It has been remarked already that nothing in the record is ever false.

Every fact required was on paper by that first nightfall: the seated posture; the patient grip; the dry-laced boots of a girl who had unlearned her fear in three weeks; the complaint fairly taken; the route advised; the lighted way. A stranger reading those pages cold, owing nothing to anyone in them, might have seen the shape in them by morning.

No stranger read them. The pages had their own readers, and the readers had their reasons, and so the shape stood in the record from the first day the way a figure stands in fog — present, patient, and mistaken at every distance for whatever the eye came hoping to find.

The books were true. The books were always true.

It was the reader that failed.

The two of them worked until the stove went cold, the old detective and the young one, hunting the paper for a man who was holding the lamp.

Outside, the snow began again, small and dry, and the bell rang the quarter hours over the North End, unhurried, the way it had for two hundred years, over the just and the unjust, the readers and the read, keeping the only watch it knew.



Chapter Four

The man who had followed Margaret Doran was found inside four days, and he was nothing.

That is written with care, because in twenty-nine years Mercer had learned that most of what a murder investigation turns up is nothing, and that the nothings have to be handled like evidence anyway, and that they break, like evidence, when handled roughly.

His name was Loomis. He clerked in a notions shop on Salem Street, thirty-one years old, a narrow polite man who lived with his mother and had, the mill girls said, been sweet on Margaret Doran in the wordless way of men who are sweet on a different girl every season and never once speak to any of them. He had taken to leaving the shop at her shift's end and walking, at a distance he must have believed respectful, the streets she walked. Three evenings a fortnight. Half a block back. Never nearer, never speaking.

In the small room at the station he sat with his hat on his knees and wept before anyone had asked him anything.

"I never," he kept saying. "I never. I only walked. A man may walk."

"A man may walk," Mercer agreed, and set about him with dates, because dates were what answered.

The dates were good for the following and bad for the murder. On the night of the twenty-sixth of February, Loomis had been at a church sociable in Charlestown from seven until eleven, in view of forty Methodists and a minister who remembered him because he had won the spelling contest and cried about that too. The fog had come down by eight. Hollis put the girl's death between eight and ten.

O'Rourke, across the table, wrote it all in his fair round hand, and when Loomis had been let out the back way — Mercer's instruction; the crowd at the rail had learned there was a man inside, and a crowd that has learned there is a man inside is already a jury — the young detective sat looking at his notes with his jaw set wrong.

"Say it," Mercer said.

"He'll be ruined anyway." O'Rourke didn't look up. "It'll be down the street by supper that he was taken in about the Doran girl. The shop will let him go by Saturday. Forty Methodists won't matter — being looked at is the verdict, for a man like that." He squared the pages, too carefully. "We did that with four days of correct work, sir. Every step right, and a man ruined at the end of it who did nothing but walk."

Mercer took his time answering, because the observation deserved it, and because it was the first time he had heard the young man arrive somewhere ahead of instruction.

"Yes," he said finally. "Suspicion is the only tool in the bag that cuts whether or not it finds anything. Remember what it costs, and spend it slow." He reached for the file. "It is also all we have. The alternative to looking at men wrongly is not looking at them at all."

He believed it when he said it. It was a true sentence in January.

There would come a night in October when he would have given the rest of his life to take it back, but that night keeps its place in this record, and the record proceeds in order.


It was Loomis, oddly, who opened the older door.

Cleared and wrecked and pathetically eager to be useful — the ruined are often eager; usefulness is the last dignity left them — he lingered at the station door on his way out and said, twisting his hat: "It's like the other ones, isn't it. The ones before."

Mercer, who had been turning away, turned back. "What ones."

"When I was a young man." Loomis blinked at being attended to. "Seventy-nine, it would have been. The winter the women were — there were three that winter, weren't there, found the same way, sitting-like. My mother wouldn't let my sisters out past the Angelus for the whole of that year. And then it stopped, and no one was ever taken for it." He looked between the two detectives, wanting to have helped. "It put me in mind, is all. The way the papers wrote this one. Sitting-like."

When he had gone, O'Rourke said, quietly, not quite as a question: "Seventy-nine."

"Before your time," Mercer said, and heard his own voice come out with a door shut in it.

O'Rourke heard the door too, and did not push on it. But walking back up the stairs he offered, carefully, in the voice of a man laying a small thing on a large table: "I know the file, sir. I've read it. When I was on nights at the old Prince Street beat there wasn't much to do at three in the morning, and the cold cases were kept in the pressroom there, and I — " he shrugged, embarrassed. "I used to read them. Yours especially. It's how I learned what a file ought to look like."

"There is no 'mine' in it," Mercer said. "The seventy-nine matter was the whole station's failure."

"Your hand is on every page of it."

"My hand is on every page of everything." He said it flatly, and it closed the subject, and they went back to the Doran file, and the Doran file, in the way of files, absorbed everything and gave nothing back.

Here the record must supply what Mercer did not say on the stairs.

Abigail Mercer died on the ninth of June, 1878, at forty-four, of the typhoid, in the sixth week. Through that autumn her husband did not sleep, and the station made allowances; and through that winter three women were strangled in the North End — a servant in November, a fish-hawker's daughter in January, a widow in March — each found seated, composed, against a wall in a narrow place, each dead by a patient narrow grip that the surgeon of that day called deliberate. Mercer worked the case as a man works who has nothing to go home to. The station said afterward that the work had saved him. He was at the pressroom all hours; he wore out two lamps; he filled four books with the finest investigation anyone in the building had ever read. It came to nothing. In the spring the killings stopped on their own, the way a fever breaks in the night, unexplained, and the city forgot with the speed of cities, and the four fine books went into the pressroom to teach patience to whatever young man might someday read them at three in the morning.

Mercer did not forget. He kept the anniversary of each of the three deaths privately, the way he kept Abigail's, in the almanac in his desk: small ruled crosses, no names. If asked — Nell had asked, once — he said a man in his line kept his failures where he could see them, because a failure you can see is a debt, and a debt can someday be paid.

That was the wound O'Rourke's careful sentence had touched, and the young man had the wit not to touch it twice.

The seventy-nine matter comes back into this record later, and at length, and it is fair — it is required — to say here what the reader will wish afterward to check: that everything just stated about it is true, and complete as far as it goes, and that "as far as it goes" is a phrase that should be watched in this book the way lamps are watched in fog.


Chapter Five

Annie Ferris came to the rail on the ninth of February, which was a Friday, at the slack hour before supper.

She was twenty-four, a laundress, big-shouldered from the work, with a temper the mangle-room respected, and she was not frightened — she wished that understood at once. She was reporting a thing because the thing was a fact, and facts about her door were her business.

Her door on Commercial Street had been opened. Not robbed — opened. Twice in a month she had come home to the latch standing off when she had left it on. Nothing gone, nothing moved but a chair, and the second time a candle burned a quarter down that she had left whole.

"Somebody's been in and sat," she said. "In my room. Sat and burned my candle and gone away, and taken nothing, and I want it written down that I said so."

It was taken down. The desk that Friday was O'Rourke's again — the rotation had it, one week in four, and the record may as well note here, because the reader is going to make something of it or fail to, that the rotation was posted at the year's start in Sergeant Byrne's hand, was public as weather, and could be consulted by anyone in the building who cared what desk any officer sat on any night of the year.

O'Rourke took her seriously — she said so afterward, to her sister; the young one wrote it all fair and never once smiled — and he advised the sensible things: a second bolt, a word with the landlord about who else held keys.

The landlord's name was Creed, and Mercer and O'Rourke paid him a visit the following week in the ordinary course, the Doran matter having gone cold enough that neighboring troubles got their turn again. Creed owned three buildings on Commercial Street and the keys to all of them, and was the sort of man who answers a question about candles with his opinion of the Irish. They left him sweating — Mercer had a manner with landlords — and left the matter open, and it would have sat in the book as one of the winter's thousand small unfinished things, except.

Except is not procedure. Mercer would have struck the word from any report a junior brought him. But this record has left procedure's jurisdiction, and the plain fact is that the Ferris entry did not sit in the book like the winter's other small things, because of what found it there.


Of Mercer's own winter, the record of those weeks shows this.

He worked the days, and taught the young man in the evenings, and did not sleep the nights, and none of the three facts was concealed from anyone. His insomnia was station legend, indulged the way the peculiarities of the very good are indulged. Since seventy-eight, the men said. He'd doze an hour at his desk at midnight, wake at one or two as if to a bell no one else heard, and then the quiet hours had him. Sometimes he read at the station. Sometimes he walked — he called it walking the beat of the books: he'd take the complaint book's fresh pages in the evening, and then walk the streets they named, at the hours they named, to see what the paper looked like standing up. He had preached it to two generations of detectives: paper answers, but you must go and stand where the paper stands. No one thought it strange. It was the most admired habit in the station.

Nell kept his house, and so Nell kept, without keeping it anywhere but in herself, the domestic ledger of those nights: the lamp in his study lit at three when she woke for water; his bed unslept-in twice a week and made smooth by his own hand to spare her the noticing, which spared nothing; his boots by the stove some mornings with the welts dark and the toes toward the heat, though she had not heard him go out nor come in, and the fog, to be fair, was that winter almost nightly, and a man who walks an hour in a Boston harbor fog comes home wet to the knee without ever meeting rain.

She was twenty-two and she loved him and the winter had three strangled women in it by springtime. The wet boots meant her father was working himself ill. That is what wet boots mean, in the house of a good man.

She made him porridge and said the true thing while pouring, as her mother would have: "You were out again."

"The Ferris door," he said, or the Doran corner, or whichever page it had been. "I wanted to stand where it stands."

"At three."

"Things stand differently at three."

"So do fathers," Nell said, and set the pot down, and let it be — because what was there to press? A man investigating strangled girls walks at night. The explanation was not only innocent, it was noble; it was the exact shape of everything she admired in him.

An explanation the exact shape of what you already admire is the hardest thing on earth to check beneath.

Nobody checked beneath. The record does not blame them. The record only sets down, in its proper winter, in its proper order, who knew what: that the station knew he never slept, and honored it; that Nell knew the boots were wet, and worried over his health; that Mercer himself knew his nights had thin places in them — hours that folded over like a page turned two at once, a dozing at the desk that ended streets from the desk, once a waking at his own cold stove with his coat on and no memory of the walk home — and that he filed these under the only headings a sane man has for them. Exhaustion. Age. Grief, which everyone knows plays tricks with time.

He noted them, though. That is the terrible and the redeeming thing, and this record will stand on it: he was Mercer even about himself. In the almanac in his desk, among the ruled crosses, there began that winter to appear now and then a small penciled circle at a date's corner. No word beside it. Just a circle — a clerk's mark for a sum that had not come out even, entered by a man who could not have said, if asked, precisely what column it belonged to, and who did not ask himself, because the nights were thin but the days were full, and in the days there was a case, and the case had all the darkness in it a man could be asked to look at.


Chapter Six

Annie Ferris was found on the thirtieth of March, a Sunday, in the areaway behind her own building on Commercial Street, seated against the coal-hatch, composed, her shawl arranged, dead since the fog of the night before.

The same narrow grip. The same patience. Hollis at the post-mortem said less than in February; there was less need; he only looked across the body at Mercer and said, "Your man again," and then, wiping his spectacles, the sentence that had been boxed once already: "He does know how long it takes. I'll say this too — " he held up his own two fingers and thumb and looked at them — "it's a spare sort of hand does this. Narrow. Your dock-walloper crushes; your big hand mauls and tears the collar. This is close work. Clerkly, almost. Put that in the file or don't."

Mercer put it in his own book. He did not, that week, put it in the file. He could not afterward account for the omission and did not try; he was a man drowning in work; a note held back a week is nothing; the record sets it down only because the record must set everything down, that being now its entire and only point.

Two dead. One grip. The city noticed.

The papers, which had given Margaret Doran a single day, gave Annie Ferris the week, and gave her the word that changes everything, the word that turns deaths into a creature: again. Women who had walked alone began going in pairs. The Angelus emptied the streets of girls the way Loomis's mother had emptied them in another winter, and men stood at the rail all day informing on their neighbors' lodgers, and Captain Deven began coming down to the detectives' room twice a day, which was his way of applying pressure and slower than a vise only in principle.

Creed the landlord was looked at hard, and looked at again — he had keys, he had the temper, and no wife would say where he was of a Saturday night. For nine days Creed was the station's man, and Mercer let the station have him, and did not quite have him himself: the keys explained the door, but the door was Ferris's alone; nothing had ever stood off its latch at Doran's lodging; and the grip — Creed's hands were a mason's, wide as stove-lids. The narrow-hand note sat in Mercer's private book like a stone in a boot. The station's man did not fit the station's dead, and Mercer said so at last, in the captain's office, and was invited warmly to produce a man who did.

That was the ledger night.

It has a place in station legend, that night — the two of them, the old detective and his student, in the empty room with one lamp, the case books open in a horseshoe and Mercer walking the inside of it in his shirtsleeves until three, building the first true architecture of the thing: the columns ruled TIME, PLACE, GRIP, POSTURE, TAKEN/NOT TAKEN; the freezing out of Creed and Loomis and the dock theory and the tramp theory; the first written statement, in the fair copy O'Rourke made of Mercer's scrawl, of the fact that would govern everything after —

Both women seated. Both composed. Nothing taken. He does not rob them and he does not outrage them and he does not hate them. He attends them. The word is chosen with care. Whatever this is, it is not fury. It is ministration.

— and, boxed at the page's foot, in Mercer's own hand, the oldest entry in his private religion, copied out now into the case itself:

Begin with what answers.

O'Rourke fell asleep toward four in the horseshoe's corner chair, mid-sentence, a young man run honestly to the end of himself, and Mercer stood a while in the lamp with the ruled pages breathing around him, and felt — he set it down days later in the almanac, one of the few soft entries in that hard little book — something he was ashamed of and could not unfeel: gratitude. Not for the deaths. Never that. For the shape of the work. For the horseshoe of open books and the columns that held, for the one lamp and the quiet, for a thing in the world large enough to require all of him. He had not slept in five years and here at last was a country where not sleeping was the qualification. He wrote: God forgive a man who is only whole inside a calamity, and ruled a line under it, and that entry the reader is asked to hold on to, as the one time in this record that Edward Mercer, with no evidence at all, described himself correctly.

He woke his student at five and sent him home to wash.

He himself did not go home. Nell, arriving down to her kitchen at six, found the porridge pot already used and scoured — he did such things; he hated to be tended — and his boots by the stove, toes to the warmth, the welts dark with wet.

She stood looking at them a moment, in the grey light.

Fog again last night, she thought. He'll walk himself into a grave.

She was twenty-two, and he was her structure, as he was the station's, as he was — for the reader has by now surely joined this household too — the reader's own. She warmed his boots and made new porridge.

In the almanac, at the corner of the thirtieth of March, there is a small penciled circle.

Nothing in the record is false.



Chapter Seven

On the first Sunday of April, Nell Mercer laid the table for three.

It had begun in March, the third place. Her father brought the young man home from church — they had taken to the same Mass, teacher and student, which the parish found touching — and Nell had fed him, and he had carved the joint when her father's wrist was bad, and thanked her in the right amount, neither courtly nor familiar, and had known enough to praise the bread and not the beef, the bread being hers and the beef being the butcher's. He was easy to have at a table. That is a talent, and a rarer one than singing.

By April it was standing custom, and the house was better for it, and she would have said so plainly to anyone who asked, because the third place at that table had been her mother's, and for five years the two of them had eaten beside its absence every Sunday like people keeping a fast, and John O'Rourke, whatever else the record must come to say of everyone, broke that fast, and there was laughter in the house on Sundays, in the year of which this record treats.

They talked shop at table, the two men, which Abigail would never have allowed, and Nell allowed, because her father at shop was her father lit. He was teaching. It poured out of him, twenty-nine years of it — how to read a roster against a man's story; how a lie sits in a page differently than an error; how to walk a street at the hour of the crime and let the street correct the file. The young man took it the way dry ground takes rain. He had a habit Nell marked because her schoolchildren had it too, the good ones: he repeated the sentences back, later in the meal, in his own order, to make sure they had lodged.

"Begin with what answers," he said once, over the pudding, trying the weight of it. "Feelings don't answer."

"Paper answers," her father finished, comfortable as a psalm.

"And when the paper's wrong, sir?"

"Paper is never wrong. Paper is only ever incomplete." Her father filled the young man's glass. "A file can fail to hold the truth. It cannot hold a falsehood and keep it — a falsehood in a file rubs against every true page it touches, and a patient reader feels the heat. Time and pages, Mr. O'Rourke. Enough of both, and the books always give the man up."

"Any man?"

"Any man."

Nell, clearing, saw her father say it, and saw the young man take it in and nod slowly and look, for a held moment, at nothing — and the record is obliged to report what she made of the look, because she thought about it after: she thought he looked like one of her boys at the school when a rule of grammar suddenly reaches back and reorders every sentence the child has ever heard. Awed, she would have said. And a little sick.

She put it to shyness and brought the coffee.


It was that same Sunday, at the door, in his coat, that O'Rourke asked his one strange thing.

Her father had gone back in for tobacco. The young man stood on the step, hat in hand, and said, not quite looking at her: "Does he sleep now? Your father. The station tells stories — since your mother, they say he doesn't. I ask because — " he turned the hat a quarter-turn — "I'm with him most hours God sends, now, and I never once see him tire. A man learns to want his teacher mortal. It would be a kindness to know he sleeps."

"He sleeps," Nell said, and heard herself defend the house the way you straighten a picture at a knock — reflex, before thought. "Badly. In pieces. He walks when it's worst — it's the work; he walks his cases. You needn't fear for him. He's iron."

"Iron," O'Rourke agreed, and put his hat on, and then, from the bottom step, with a lightness she remembered afterward as slightly too well made: "The walks did him no harm in the old winters, I suppose. Well. Good night, Miss Mercer. Thank the house for me."

She stood in the doorway while he went off up the hill, a young man walking easily in the last of the light, and behind her in the parlor her father hummed at the tobacco jar, at rest, at anchor, home; and if the reader has been taught by other books to hear a cold wind in the young man's question, the reader is free to hear it; Nell heard a student who loved his master, asking the daughter to say the master would live forever.

Both readings are recorded. The record does not choose between them.

Not yet.


Chapter Eight

The record steps back now, fifteen days, to fetch a thing that was in the book the whole time.

On the fourteenth of March — a fortnight before Annie Ferris died, when the city still thought it had one murder and one only — a dressmaker's assistant named Clara Whitfield came to the rail at the slack of the afternoon, bought a place in the queue with an hour of her half-day, and reported as follows, which is copied fair from the complaint book:

Mar. 14. Clara Whitfield, 21, of Fleet St., dressmaker's asst. States a man has twice alighted behind her from the Hanover St. horse-car of an evg. and kept behind her to her door. Dark coat, dark hat, could not swear to the face. Requests it be written down. Entry taken at the rail.

The reader will want the desk that afternoon, and shall have it: the fourteenth was a Wednesday in O'Rourke's rotation week, and the hand is his, fair and round, the seventeenth entry of a long day.

Clara Whitfield was one of nine young women who came to that rail between January and June of that year with troubles of the following kind — the reader shall have that number too, and is asked to sit with it: nine. A city is a loud place full of men, and its women file their fear at a steady rate, and in the complaint book of the Hanover Street station the fourteenth of March looks exactly like the eleventh of January and the ninth of February look — one frightened entry among many, correctly taken, reasonably advised, signed, dated, shelved.

Three of the nine are in this book. Six are not. If the six had a mark upon them that spared them, or the three a mark that chose them, no reader of the book has ever found it — no reader save one, and what he used for a mark, and when, and in which hours, is the matter of Part Three, and will keep.

She was advised at the rail as the others were advised: vary the route, keep to the lamps, walk with a friend from the car stop. And — the record having promised to show its work — she was walked out to the steps, as Margaret Doran had been walked, and the lighted way pointed out to her, because the young officer at the desk did such things, on his rotation, for the frightened ones; it was the small extra unpaid courtesy that made him worth marking for better; and each of the nine women got some portion of that courtesy, the spared six and the taken three alike, so that the courtesy, examined afterward from every side by men desperate to convict it, proves out even, and innocent, and no mark at all.

The shop on Hanover took the rest of her half-days that spring. Clara was quick with a needle and quicker with the shop's accounts, and the proprietress — a Mrs. Verey, squared and exact, of whom more later — had begun to let her keep the order-book, which Clara ruled at night in a hand her mother in Salem called her fortune. Her letters home that spring are in this record's possession. They are a good girl's letters: the ribbon she was saving for; the sermon; the price of thread; her mother not to worry — that refrain in every one, her mother not to worry, the follower had left off since the police were told (he had; the horse-car man proved out later to be a married engraver with reasons of his own for the same stop, squalid and small and nothing worse); the streets were watched now; the girls went in pairs.

The last letter is dated the twenty-eighth of April and was never posted. It sat sealed on her bedside table beside her Bible, stamped, addressed, waiting for the Monday, because postage being what it was she wrote through the week and sent on Mondays.

It reaches Salem later in this record, carried by hand.

It is the letter with the sentence in it that the reader of this book already knows, if the reader has come the whole way round; and if the reader has not, it will keep, as everything in a ledger keeps.

April moved on the city. Ferris was a fortnight dead, then three weeks; Creed the landlord was let go back to his keys and his opinions; the pairs of walking girls loosened, as pairs do, the fear wearing thin where it rubbed against wages and weather and the plain human impossibility of being afraid every evening at six. The Angelus filled again. Mrs. Verey's shop worked late on the spring orders, and on the last Saturday of the month the walking-friend who shared Clara's way home was kept back with a toothache, gone by noon to the drawer of a dentist on Salem Street.

The twenty-ninth of April was a Sunday.

The bell began before the light.


Chapter Nine

It did not ring sharply. It rolled.

Iron against iron, deliberate and unhurried, the sound leaving the tower of the Old North and settling over the parish in slow waves, over the docks and the boardinghouses and the empty Sunday yards, and a scrubwoman named Mrs. Cahill, late for the six o'clock, gathered her skirts on Hanover Street and cut through the alley between the two boardinghouses, as she did every Sunday of her life, and nearly stepped into the figure before she saw it.

The woman sat against the far wall as if resting.

"Miss," Mrs. Cahill called gently, being herself a woman who had slept cold in doorways in a hard year once. "You'll freeze."

The woman did not stir. The rest Mrs. Cahill did as the record already holds: the step closer; the sleeve; the cold; the head shifting slightly with gravity; the bruising, thumb-shaped, finger-shaped; the neighbor of her own age seated dead in the Sunday bells. She ran, and her running raised the street, and the street's word ran downhill to the station faster than any wagon.

Detective Mercer was at the alley by seven.

He had been at the station since five — the record continues to show its work; the desk sergeant logged him in, red-eyed, at eight minutes past, come to walk the morning's pages as was his custom, the fog of the night just lifting. He came up Hanover at a pace that scattered pigeons, and the crowd at the alley mouth parted for him the way crowds did, and he stepped inside and stood still, and whatever the onlookers expected of the great man at such a moment, what he did was remove his hat.

He was a long time crouched over her, and the men let him be.

Young woman. Early twenties. Gloves still on. No signs of struggle. Seated, composed, attended — his own word from the ledger night, standing up now in brick and cold flesh for the third time, and he crouched in the sound of the bells with his hat in his hand and read the scene the way other men of that parish were at that hour reading their missals.

Mud darkened the lower hem of her dress. He noticed it, then set it aside. April made mud of half the city.

"Third district's already restless," said a voice at the alley mouth.

O'Rourke.

The record sets down exactly what the older man observed, because he was a machine for observing and not even that morning could stop the machine: that his junior had arrived from the wrong direction, north, though his lodgings lay south; that he had arrived unsummoned — no runner had been sent yet, the wagon not called; that his coat was wrinkled at the shoulders and his collar poorly settled, a man dressed fast or dressed long ago; that he had about him the particular stale-eyed wakefulness that Mercer knew better than any man in Boston, the look of a night not slept; and that he came up the alley too quickly and crouched at the dead girl's hem too near, his hand half-rising toward the mud.

"Careful," Mercer said.

"It's fresh."

"So is the morning."

The younger man glanced up, embarrassed, but did not stop thinking; and here the record must be scrupulous, because what he said next was to travel a long way in this book, wearing first one coat and then a very different one:

"She doesn't fit here," O'Rourke said. "Woman dressed for morning, gloves on, shoes clean but the hem. She didn't wander in. She was brought — or — " and he paused, the pause of a man hearing his own thought a half-second late, "— or expected. Expected to pass. He didn't bring her to the place. He put the place where she'd be."

Mercer let the sentence hang long enough to test it, and found it held his weight.

"Say the second half again."

"He put the place where she'd be." O'Rourke stood, wiping his palm on his coat though it had touched nothing. "He knew her route, sir. Like Doran. Like — " He stopped. His face did something Mercer filed without name. "Like a man who reads."

Down the alley, in the bells, the two detectives looked at each other over the seated dead girl, and the crowd at the mouth watched them the way congregations watch, and somebody in it asked aloud how many now, and Mercer straightened, and put his hat back on, and gave the crowd the sentence the city required of him.

"This will be handled."

He believed it. That is the last time the record can write that sentence plainly, so let it stand whole: he believed it. He stood in the ringing morning over the third seated woman of that year — the sixth of his life, though the record alone is counting — with his method intact and his student sharp beside him and his city frightened behind him, and he believed, with the whole of the man he understood himself to be, that order would do what order had always done.

Boston had order.

And order always returned.

The bells finished the hour and left the alley in quiet, and in the quiet Mercer took out his book and began, in a fair strong hand, at the top of a clean page, the file that would end everything.

He ruled the columns first. He always ruled the columns first.


END OF PART ONE



PART TWO — HOUSEKEEPING


Chapter Ten

The boardinghouse stood three streets from the alley, and inside, the warmth carried the smell of cabbage, soap, and damp wool.

"You're the detective," the landlady said.

"Edward Mercer."

"Mrs. Halcombe. I keep the house." She said it the way a captain says a ship's name, and led them up herself, and stood in the door of the small room with her hands folded while the two men read it, because it was her house and her dead lodger, and she would witness what was done in both their names.

The room was small and orderly. A workbasket squared on the chair. A Sunday dress laid ready on the bed — laid out the evening before, for the morning that had come for everyone in the parish but her. A Bible on the bedside table, and beside the Bible a sealed letter, stamped, addressed in a clean sloped hand: Mrs. E. Whitfield, Salem.

"She wrote of a Sunday evening and posted of a Monday," Mrs. Halcombe said. "Every week, faithful. Her half-dollar was never late and her lamp was never lit past ten." The folded hands tightened on each other. "You'll be wanting to open that."

"It's her mother's," Mercer said. "It will be opened by her mother."

He carried it to Salem himself, on the Tuesday, on the morning train, and this record has already once described that parlor with the blinds half drawn, and the marshes from the train window, and the crow holding its place in the wind, and sets down now only what belongs to the fuller account.

Clara's mother read her daughter's letter twice, with the detective sitting hat on knee like a caller, the clock loud between them. Then she handed it back across.

"Read it, if it helps you catch him."

Most of it was a good girl's letter, as her letters were. The ribbon, priced and postponed. Thread. The spring orders. Mrs. Verey trusting her with the order-book now — underlined, her one boast. And near the end, in the sentence written to close a mother's fear as gently as a lamp is trimmed:

"You are not to worry about the walking, Mother. There has been trouble here, but it is watched now — a kind officer walks the girls home when the light goes, and the streets are safer than ever they were. We are well looked after."

Mercer read the sentence three times on the train home, and boxed it in his mind, and could not have said why his hand was cold.

A kind officer. No name. Girls in the plural; some officer, any officer; the beats had been doubled since March, by his own recommendation; a dozen helmets walked those streets at dusk, and walking a frightened seamstress toward her door was exactly what he had asked the doubled beats to be. The sentence, read properly, was a testimonial to the doubled watch. It was his own police, doing their office. It was the system, working.

We are well looked after.

The marshes went by. The crow held its place.

He wrote in his book, under the boxed sentence: Establish which officers walked which girls, which streets, which weeks — routine elimination, and looked at the word elimination a long moment, and then out the window, and the record notes, because the record has taken the measure of its man by now, that Detective Mercer did not once in that hour frame the question any reader of this book has already framed. The question with a face in it.

You do not audit your own church for the devil.

Not first.


Mrs. Verey he saw on the Wednesday, in the shop on Hanover among the chalk and the pressed wool, and she answered like a woman folding cloth, exact and squared, having cried before he came and being done with it.

Saturday last, the walking-friend gone by noon to the dentist. Clara kept to the finish of the spring orders — her own choice, she was proud, you could not send that girl home with work standing — and left at dusk, alone, and Mrs. Verey had watched her from the window as far as the corner.

"She stopped there," Mrs. Verey said. "Someone spoke to her. She went along with him easy — down Hanover, the way she'd go anyway. I thought nothing. I turned back to the cutting-table. That's the whole of what I have, and I'll be hearing that bell over it the rest of my life, so ask your questions quick."

"The man."

"Dusk, and the width of the street." She squared an edge that was square. "A dark coat. A tall way of standing. He stood — " and here she stopped folding, and looked for the true thing, and found it, and the record asks the reader to take this sentence slowly, because every person in this book will spend the rest of it inside her next words — "he stood like the law. That's all I can swear. Not his face, not his years, not so much as whether there was a helmet on him — dusk eats all that. But a woman knows the stance. He stood like a man whose standing there was proper. Like the street was his to stand in. And so I thought nothing, and turned away." Her hands lay still on the cloth at last. "You don't look at the law, Mr. Mercer. That's what it's for. You see the shape of it and you stop looking."

Mercer wrote it fair, thanked her, and stood on Hanover Street a while in the falling afternoon with the shop bell dying behind him.

He stood like the law.

Around him the street ran its errands. A helmet passed on the far walk and touched its brim to him — he was known — and the crowd parted around the officer without looking, the way water parts around a piling, and closed behind him, and Mercer watched it happen, the little miracle of trust that every uniform swims in, and felt the case turn beneath him like a ship coming onto a new heading, slow, enormous, and cold.

The man they hunted was not hiding from the trust.

He was dressed in it.


Chapter Eleven

The city council met on the first Thursday of May, and what it wanted was a door that closes.

The record will be brief with politics, as Mercer was. Three women dead in one parish inside ninety days; the papers now printing maps with crosses on them, which sold; a delegation of North End clergy; an alderman with a nephew on the force explaining detection to the mayor; and through it all Captain Deven moving like a pilot in shoal water, buying his detectives sea-room with one hand and signing it away with the other.

"They want an arrest," he told Mercer, door closed, after. "I have told them we are near one."

"We are not."

"I know what we are." Deven sat back. "I have bought you to Whitsun, Mercer. After that the case gets help — a board, the state men in, some blue committee of the whole. You have seen what committees detect." He turned a paper on the desk, squaring it, not reading it. "Where are you. Truly."

"Closer than I can prove and further than you'd like." Mercer chose the words as a man counts out exact coin. "He is not a stranger to them, Captain. He is not a stranger to anyone. Three women who filed at our rail. Streets that were watched. Girls who went easy, in the open, at dusk, with the whole parish told to fear — no dragging, no crying out, gloves on, dressed for company. Whoever he is, women at their most frightened walk toward him." He laid Mrs. Verey's folded sentence on the desk like a specimen. "He stands like the law."

Deven read it, and was still a while, and the record credits him here: he was a political animal, but he was not a fool, and he saw the whole shape of what that sentence could mean for his force, his career, and his city, in the time it took him to read eleven words.

"A man impersonating an officer," he said at last, evenly. "Some discharged man, some watchman, some theatrical with a coat. That is the reading I can live beside, Detective, and I'd counsel you to exhaust it before you so much as dream the other one aloud. You take my meaning."

"I take it."

"Because the other reading," Deven said, and stopped, and did a thing Mercer had never seen him do in twenty years of shared rooms: he got up, and looked out his own window at his own street, with his hands behind him, and said the rest to the glass. "I have given thirty-four years to teaching this parish that the coat can be trusted. They spit on us when I came up, Mercer. Threw slops. I have buried men to buy that trust. If you put it about — on a dressmaker's dusk-sighting — that the coat itself is doing this, you will not catch a killer, you will burn a bridge that took a generation to build, and every woman in Boston will be less safe on the far side of it. The trust is the thing, man. The trust is the whole cathedral. Mind what you swing in it."

The record sets that speech down entire because it is the book's whole tragedy in a captain's Thursday growl: every word of it true, every word of it the killer's fortress, and no man alive able to tell the difference that spring — least of all the one whose case it was, who walked home the long way that evening through the cathedral of trust, past the helmets and the partings of crowds, being himself its most believed pillar; and who wrote that night in the almanac, under a small penciled circle already at the date's corner:

D. is right. The trust is the thing.

And under it, ruled off, in the smaller hand he used for entries he did not intend to reread:

But the trust is also the weapon. God help the man who finally sees which officer the frightened walk toward easiest — he will have found the killer or the best man in Boston, and I no longer know the test that tells them apart.

Nothing in the record is false.

Some entries are just written by men who cannot yet afford to know what they know.


Chapter Twelve

Loomis lost the notions shop the week Clara Whitfield died.

Not for cause. Trade was off, the proprietor said, which was true the way weather is true — and the weather around Loomis had been made in a small room at the Hanover Street station in January and had followed him out the back door and up Salem Street and into the shop and stood behind him at the counter ever since, breathing on the customers. Being looked at is the verdict, O'Rourke had said. By May the sentence was served daily. Boys called the house. His mother's priest called too, kindly, twice, which was worse.

Mercer went up the stairs to the two rooms over the chandler's on a Friday evening, unannounced, alone, and to this day the record cannot fully account for the visit — it was not procedure; the man was cleared; a detective climbing those stairs re-lit the very weather he climbed to regret. Call it the ledger conscience: Mercer paid his debts, and suspicion was a debt, and he had spent it on this man and meant to acknowledge the account.

Loomis received him with a terrible eagerness, cleared the good chair, made tea his mother could not spare.

"I don't blame the force," he said, before anything. "You had your work. I've said so on the street, Mr. Mercer, I say it every time: the police were correct with me. Correct." He held the word up like a testimonial from an employer who had also dismissed him.

Mercer drank the unsparable tea and let the man talk, and the man talked — the shop, the boys, his mother's nerves — and then, because the ruined are eager and eagerness hunts for coin, Loomis leaned forward with the air of a man producing his one remaining thing of value.

"I've been trying to help, you know. In my way. I walk in the evenings still — " a flinch — "different streets now, of course. But I had my walks all that winter, and I've been going back over them, in my mind, for anything. For you." He wet his lips. "And there's a thing I never told in January, because in January it was nothing, and I was frightened, and it would have gone against me."

"Tell it now."

"The nights I walked her way." He would not say the name; he never said the name. "Twice — three times, may be — there was another man."

The room went very quiet. The tea clicked once against its saucer.

"Where."

"Charter Street. Across from her lodging, in the mouth of the yard there, where the fog pools. I took him the first time for a lamplighter waiting on his hour, but he'd no pole, and the second time I knew him for the same because of the stillness. That was the whole mark of him, sir — the stillness. A man in a doorway of an evening shifts his feet, looks at the traffic, has business with his pipe. This one stood the way — " Loomis, who had spent his poor cramped life not being looked at, searched for it — "the way furniture stands. Hours, may be. Watching her windows, I thought — but gravely, like it was work. Like a man keeping an account."

"Describe him."

"Fog, and I never once dared pass close." Loomis's hands worked. "Tall. Never young — there was grey to him, or the fog put it there. A long dark coat, old-fashioned in the skirts. And when her lamp went out — this is the thing I'd swear, this is the thing that frighted me off the street — when her lamp went out, he'd go. Not before. The light was what he was waiting on. Her being safe down for the night. And I thought — " and here the ruined man laughed, one note, at himself, at everything — "God forgive me, sir, I thought: she has a guardian. I thought he was one of yours. A plainclothes man, set to watch over her on account of her complaint. That's why I never told it. It would have sounded mad from me — the follower complaining of a follower. And besides — " the eagerness guttered; what was left was only the small true voice of the ruined, which this record has no choice but to let stand as the last word of the chapter, over the tea his mother could not spare —

"Besides. He looked like the one who belonged there. It was me that looked like the crime."


Chapter Thirteen

The man who answered the city's need was produced in the third week of May, and his name was Daniel Flynn.

The record must walk carefully here, for it is walking on a living man.

He was thirty-four, a dock casual, big through the hands and shoulders, no fixed lodging since the spring, a drinker between jobs and sometimes during them. He had been seen on Commercial Street in March — he was seen on every street of that parish in every month; he slept in its yards. He had once shouted after a woman near the Ferris building, drunk, words no witness could improve upon under oath. He had no account of his nights that any man could check, because men who sleep in yards keep no almanacs.

O'Rourke found him. The record is exact: it was honest work, days of it, done with the diligence the young man brought to everything — statements gathered, sightings mapped, the March shout run to ground through four removes of hearsay. He built the file the way his teacher had taught him files were built, and brought it to his teacher with the pride of the student, and the file was good.

It was good the way a well-ruled column of wrong figures is good.

Mercer read it twice and sat with it, and the honest ledger of that evening — for the almanac got an entry — records the exact grain of his failure, and the reader shall have it unsoftened: he saw the holes at once. Flynn's hands, first. Stove-lids, like Creed's — and the note in Mercer's private book, Hollis's narrow clerkly grip, sat unshared and unspoken, and Mercer looked at the biggest hole in the file and did not put his student's eye to it. Second, the manner: a yard-sleeping drinker who shouted at women in daylight was fury and ruin and noise, and the man in the alleys was silence and patience and ministration; Mercer knew — knew as craft, past argument — that the grammar of Flynn was wrong from the first line.

And he signed the warrant application anyway, and the almanac says why, in one line he did not rule off, because he did not intend to hide from it:

Signed for F. today. The city must have someone, or it will begin looking properly.

The reader is asked to stop at that sentence, as Mercer, to his credit and his damnation, stopped at it himself — pen down, lamp low, a long time.

Then he closed the book, and the machinery took Daniel Flynn.

The arrest was made at the Eastern Avenue yards on the twentieth of May, and the city put its flags out inside its heart. The papers had the face by Thursday — and it was the right face for the story, which is a terrible sentence to have to write: heavy, unshaven, baffled, exactly the shape that fear had been drawing on its wall all spring. The maps with crosses stopped selling. The pairs of walking girls loosened gladly. The alderman's nephew was mentioned favorably in three sheets, Deven aged backward five years in a fortnight, and at the Hanover Street station the rail grew quiet of a sudden, the complaints of frightened women dropping away to almost none — which every man in the building took for the surest sign of all that the right door had closed.

Every man but two.

O'Rourke — and the record gives the young man his due here in full, for it will cost him so much later — O'Rourke, who had built the file, began to doubt it the week it succeeded. It was the quiet at the rail that worked on him first: "They've stopped filing," he said, at the horseshoe table, at night. "The women. Why would the frightened stop filing because we've arrested a man? Fear doesn't read the papers that fast. Unless — " and he stopped, and Mercer watched him walk to the edge of a thought and look over it — "unless the ones who'd have filed were only ever frightened of a thing that's still loose, and know it, somehow, low down. You can feel it at the rail, sir. They come to the steps now and turn back. It's as if the building's changed to them."

"The building is the same," Mercer said.

"Then something that lives in it isn't," said O'Rourke, meaning nothing, meaning a mood, a spring, a season — and wrote up his night's notes in his fair round hand, while across the horseshoe his teacher sat with a stillness upon him that the young man, absorbed, did not remark.

The way furniture stands.

And Mercer — Mercer in those first Flynn weeks was, the station agreed, a man visibly unburdened. He slept — actually slept; Nell heard it and wept once in the kitchen with relief; the boots stood dry by the stove a fortnight together. He was patient in the corridors, generous at the Sunday table. Deven, meeting him on the stairs, told him he looked ten years the younger man, and meant it, and Mercer thanked him, and the record closes the chapter on the one entry the almanac holds from that whole serene and lethal fortnight, the smallest entry in the book, four words, unruled, uncircled:

The quiet holds. Why?

Nothing in the record is false.

But some questions are locks, and a man may carry one twenty years in his pocket, taking it out, turning it over, admiring the workmanship of it — and die without once conceding that he had also, all along, and in the same pocket, been carrying the key.



Chapter Fourteen

Kate Flynn came to the rail for the first time on the last Monday of May, and after that she came every Monday, the way other women went to a grave.

She was two years the prisoner's elder, in service in Roxbury, and she spent her one afternoon a week riding in to stand at the rail of the station that had taken her brother and say, to whoever could not avoid her, the same four sentences. He never hurt a woman in his life. He shouted, drink shouting, and shame on him, and I've boxed his ears for it myself. But he sleeps in yards because our mother died owing, and he gives his day's pay to any child that asks, and he never hurt a woman in his life. Ask anyone on the docks that isn't afraid of you.

The station learned to be busy when she came. Mercer never once managed it. She had marked him for the senior man in her first week, and thereafter she aimed her four sentences at him with the terrible patience of the powerless, and he stood for it, hat in hand, Monday upon Monday, because a man pays his debts and he knew a debt when it stood at a rail looking at him.

"You signed for him," she said once. Not loud. She was never loud. "They tell me it was the young one built the case, but the old one signed. You're the old one."

"I am."

"Then you'll be the one it's on," Kate Flynn said, "when it comes out wrong," and gathered her shawl and went to catch the Roxbury car, leaving the sentence in him like a needle worked deep, where it stayed, and worked, as needles do.


It came out wrong, privately, on the eighth of June.

O'Rourke did it — of course O'Rourke did it; the record has shown the pattern of the young man and the reader shall have it plain: he could not leave a wrong figure alone, even his own; especially his own. Doubting his file since the quiet at the rail, he had gone back to first principles the way he'd been taught — begin with what answers; the nights are what answer; account for the man's nights — and where every account failed for want of paper, he had at last thought of the one institution that papers the nights of men who sleep in yards.

The Deer Island workhouse logged its lockups by name and date.

Daniel Flynn was on its February page. Taken up drunk on the Charlestown bridge the twenty-fifth, delivered to the island the same night, discharged the morning of the twenty-eighth. On the night Margaret Doran died in Prince Street, the man arrested for her murder was in a locked ward in the middle of Boston Harbor, initialed in at seven and counted twice before dawn.

O'Rourke put the certified copy on Mercer's desk without a word, and stood while he read it, white as the paper.

"I built it," he said. "I built it and it was nothing. Worse — it was backwards. Sir, I have to know what I did wrong or I'm no use to you. Where was the flaw in the method?"

"The method was sound. The question was wrong." Mercer laid the copy down. He was aware of his own calm the way a man is aware of a bandage: something between him and a thing that would otherwise be felt. "We asked, of a frightened city, who looks like this? — and the city answered, and the answer looked splendid, because that is the one question a frightened city always answers splendidly." He tapped the certificate. "You have just asked, of a locked ledger, where was this man on the night? Paper's question. Paper's answer. Mark the difference and you've paid for the lesson."

"Then he's out." The young man's jaw was set. "Today. We take this up and he's out by supper."

"He is not," Mercer said, "and you had better hear it from me standing than from the captain sitting," and told him what the reader shall hear in the next chapter from Deven entire; and watched his student take, for the first time, the taste of the thing itself — the thing under the case, under the force, under the whole ruled and columned world; and watched him not swallow it.

"It's wrong," O'Rourke said. Simply. The way the young say it, before cost.

"Yes."

"You'll fight it."

"I will do," Mercer said slowly, "what can be done in the order it can be done," and heard, in his own mouth, the exact cadence of the answers that had been given to him up every staircase of his twenty-nine years, and hated it, and gave it anyway; and the record marks this evening, the eighth of June, as the night the student stopped writing down everything the teacher said.


It was also the week of the second conversation with Hollis, which the record has been holding, and holds no longer.

The surgeon caught him on the mortuary stairs. "That note of mine ever make its way to your file? The hands." He held up his own spare pair, wiggled them. "Narrow, patient, clerkly. I ask because I read in the sheets that your man Flynn is built along the lines of a coal barge, and it has been sitting on me at nights."

"The note is in my book."

"Your book." Hollis looked at him over the spectacles, a long, mild, surgeon's look, the kind that goes through skin on its way to the matter. "Twenty years we've worked, Mercer, and I've known your book to be ahead of your file exactly never. What's it doing there?"

And Edward Mercer, who did not lie — the record stakes itself on this; the man did not lie, it was half of what he was, the ruled half — stood on the stairs and heard himself say: "Waiting until it can do good instead of harm. Half the force fits your note, Doctor. I'll not start a hand-measuring panic in my own station house on the strength of a bruise."

Which was true.

Which was reasonable.

Which was, the reader is invited to observe — as Mercer himself observed, later, in a room this record has not yet reached — the first time in twenty-nine years that Edward Mercer had a piece of paper that answered, and kept it from the case.

Half the force fits the note, he had said.

In his own desk, in his own hand, in the almanac beside the ruled crosses of 1879, the entry for that night reads, whole:

H. asked after his note. I gave him policy. The true answer is that I have begun to be afraid of the note, and cannot find the man in me who will say of what.

A small penciled circle sits at the corner of the date.

It is the first circle that year to sit beside words.


Chapter Fifteen

Deven's door was closed for eleven minutes on the ninth of June, and the station outside it developed urgent business at the corridor's far end, because the voices carried anyway.

The record has the substance from both men and sets it down as spoken.

"He was on Deer Island in February. It's certified, Captain. He cannot have done Doran."

"Then he did the others." Deven's voice was flat as a wall. "Or he did none and his lawyer will make hay of the island in the fall, and a jury will let him go, and that will be the system operating as built, at the proper season, through the proper door. What you are asking me for is June."

"I am asking you for an innocent man's summer."

"You are asking me to stand on the steps of this house and tell the North End that the man we gave them is nothing, that the streets are as they were, that it is all to do again — on the strength of a workhouse ledger, in an election year, with the trust of the parish balanced on this force like a dinner service on one arm." A chair pushed back. "Frightened cities don't want the truth, Mercer. They want a door that closes. I have given them one. It holds the fear out. While it holds I can keep this parish policed by consent instead of by riot, and if the price of that is one dock rat's uncomfortable summer at the city's board and lodging, I will pay it and sleep."

"And when it happens again? While your door is shut on the wrong man?"

Silence, the length of two breaths.

"Then it's a new case," said Captain Deven. "That's how order is kept."

"That isn't order. It's housekeeping."

"They are usually the same thing," Deven said, weary as scripture, "and you have known it longer than I have," and the door opened, and Mercer came out through the corridor's sudden industry with his face closed, and went up to the detectives' room, and did not slam the ruled world down on the desk, or resign, or go to the papers, or do any of the things that men do in the stories cities tell themselves about their honest servants.

He went back to work. Both kinds.

The record means the sentence to be read twice.


Chapter Sixteen

The quiet of that May broke, in the second week of June, the way ice breaks — from underneath.

It is all in the almanac, and the record from here forward will lean upon that small hard book more and more, because the events of the summer ran in two ledgers, and the almanac is where the second one kept its accounts without knowing it.

The sleep went first. The serene fortnight — the station's phrase, afterward, always with the shudder in it — ended as it had begun, without cause. The third place at the Sunday table had gone silent and correct since the eighth of June; the student came, still, but came now the way a man visits a sick house, and the teacher poured the wine and the two of them made conversation over the body of the thing between them, and Nell watched her house go dark again by inches and could not name the day it happened.

The lamp at three returned. The walking returned — he called it the old name, walking the beat of the books, and it was; the record insists; it was; he took the complaint book's pages in the evening and walked their streets at their hours, a detective at his known and admired work; the pages he chose were the pages of the nine women, the filed and frightened, the spared six — checking, he told himself, the doubled beats; auditing the trust; establishing, as his own note had put it, which officers walked which girls — and if a man stands long enough in the mouth of a yard where the fog pools, watching a lodging-house window for the lamp to go safely out, with stillness the whole mark of him, then the difference between a guardian and the other thing is a difference no witness on earth could swear to.

Loomis could have told him that.

The boots are in the almanac. The reader has been promised the pebble and shall have it exact. The dry spell that June ran nine days, the harbor fogs failing, the streets parched and the water-carts out — it is in the weather columns of every sheet in the city, the ninth to the seventeenth, no rain, no fog, remarked on by everyone.

The entry for the morning of the sixteenth of June reads:

Boots wet through at the welts. Rimed white at the seams when dried — salt. No fog these six nights. No memory of the harborside. N. must not see the boots.

And then, ruled off, in the small hand:

I have begun to keep a fact from my own daughter. File that where it belongs. The nights have thin places and I no longer believe exhaustion covers the account. Something walks me. I write it so: something walks me to the water and stands me there and brings me home wet, and the man who is walked remembers the inside of the fog and nothing else — a bell somewhere, iron, unhurried. I am a detective, God help me. I know what I would say of these entries were they another man's. I would say: begin with what answers. Account for his nights.

I cannot account for my nights.

The circle at that date is pressed so deep the pencil broke, and was mended, and the entry goes on — and the record gives the last of it without comment, because there is no comment; there is only the man, at his own horseshoe table at last, with his own name in the space where the suspect goes, doing what he then did:

But the days are whole. The work is true. A mind under grief and no sleep for five years will walk, and dream walking, and salt is on every stone of this city if a man goes near the water, and I was on the harborside Tuesday in the plain day. There is a case, and it is at its crisis, and it will not be solved by an old man frighting himself with his own boots at six in the morning. The thin nights are a medical matter. I will see to them when Flynn is out and the true man taken. Until then: the days are whole. Begin with what answers. The case answers.

The case answers.

Six days later there was a new page for it to answer with.


Chapter Seventeen

Bridget Moore was twenty-three, a hood-and-mantle maker, and she lived up two flights in Paull's Court off Salem Street with a lamp she read by late, being a great one for the story papers.

She had come to the rail on the fourth of June — a Wednesday evening, the desk sergeant's own week, Byrne's careful schoolroom hand — and what she reported was a man in the archway.

Paull's Court had one entrance, an arched passage from Salem Street, and for three nights running, waking thirsty or restless in the small hours, she had seen from her window a figure standing in it. Not loitering — she was clear on the distinction, Byrne remembered; a loiterer moves, smokes, minds the street. This one stood. Tall, a long dark coat, greyish about the head or else the lamplight made him so, face never once raised into the light. Still as the arch itself. "Like furniture," she told Byrne, who wrote it down smiling a little at the phrase, and the smile is on his conscience yet. The first night she took him for a watchman. The second for a drunk holding up the wall. The third night the stillness came home to her as its own kind of wrongness — nothing stands like that, she told Byrne, that isn't waiting on something — and when her lamp was out and she looked again from the dark, the archway was empty.

He waited on the light, Byrne repeated at the inquiry, grey-faced, months on. That's what she'd marked most. He went when her lamp went out. I wrote it all fair, gentlemen. Every word. It was in the book.

It was in the book.

She was found on the twenty-second of June, a Sunday morning again — the bells again; the parish had begun to cross itself at its own bells — in the narrow court itself, ten steps from her stair door, seated against the arch's inner wall.

But wrong.

Mercer knew it for wrong from the passage mouth, before he had crossed half the court, and the record has the wrongness in his own scene-notes, jotted standing: Purse gone. Gloves gone — taken from the hands, buttons burst. Bruising broad at the throat, forearm-wide, torn collar. Rings gone, finger scored. Robbery, says the scene. The scene has never said robbery in five months. Why does the scene say robbery?

Hollis answered by Tuesday, in the flat voice he kept for the worst things.

"Two hands were at that girl, Mercer, or one hand in two moods. Underneath, first in time: your man. The narrow grip, either side, patient, sustained — she was dead by it, same as the others, no error. Then, after death — minutes after, no more — the rest was done. Collar torn, throat bruised broad with something that was not fingers — a forearm, gloved, laid on and dragged. Rings pulled. Purse cut, not slipped. It is robbery painted over ministration, and painted coarse." He set down the spectacles at last. "Your man has learned shame, or caution, or both. He is dressing his work as something else. And I will tell you the one detail that he could not bring himself to spoil, because I think it will mean more to you than to me. He tore her collar and scored her finger and burst her glove-buttons — and then he sat her up. Against the wall. Decent. Composed. Attended, you called it once." The surgeon looked at him across the sheeted girl. "Whatever it is in him that arranges them, Mercer, it is stronger than his fear of you. He could counterfeit everything but the kindness."

Mercer went back to the station through streets already boiling — a fourth, while their man sat jailed; Deven's door had bought exactly the summer it had bought — and he went in past the rail without his hat, and took down the complaint book, because his mind had been in it all the way from Paull's Court: the archway, the stillness, the watcher who left when the lamp went out. Filed, Byrne had said, sick. Fourth of June. I took it myself.

He turned to the fourth of June.

The page was gone.

Not torn — torn leaves ragged witness. Cut. Razored close in against the binding, a clean quarter-inch stub, the work of an unhurried hand and a good blade, and the book sewn tight enough that only a man looking for the seam would ever see the seam; the entries ran on across the gap, third of June to fifth, and the book lay flat and honest-looking and violated in his two hands, at his own rail, in his own station, under the eyes of his own force.

The court has one arch, he thought, with the clarity that comes to a man at the top of a long fall. She told us where he stands. She filed the killer himself, and the file has been bled.

And then the second clarity, the one the whole spring had been building toward, joist by joist, and it went through the station's evening din like the cold goes through a coat:

Cut from inside the rail.

No public hand could have done it. The book lived behind the rail, under Byrne's eye by day, locked to the outer room by night — locked in, that is, with the building's own men. Whoever had bled the page had stood where officers stand, in the hours when officers own the room, with a razor and the calm to use it slowly, eleven steps from the cells.

The pattern was not in the streets. It had never been in the streets.

It was in this book. It filed at this rail. It was written down in a fair hand and shelved behind him while he taught, and it read the frightened at their most exact and current and confiding — read them the way he had preached the book should be read, line by line, in the quiet hours, when the city lay still on the page and a patient man could walk through it.

Mercer stood at the rail with the wounded book open in his hands, and around him the station worked and swore and slammed its doors, his station, his men, his cathedral of trust with its thousand coats hanging on its pegs — and the record closes Part Two on the sentence his scene-notes close on that night, boxed, in a hand pressed hard enough to read from the page's back, the sentence the reader has been walking toward since a mill girl twisted her gloves at this same rail in January, though reader and writer walked toward it hunting different men:

He is one of us.


END OF PART TWO



PART THREE — THE NARROWING


Chapter Eighteen

He told no one for nine days, and the nine days must be accounted for, because they were the loneliest work of his life and the record will not have them mistaken for hesitation.

A detective who believes the killer wears the coat cannot use the coat's machinery to hunt him. Every order Mercer might give could be the order that warned him; every man he might trust could be the man; the case had become a surgery to be performed on the surgeon's own body, and the first thing Mercer did was what any reader of this record will by now expect of him.

He started a second set of books.

Not at the station. At home, in the locked drawer below the almanac: fresh ledgers, bought with coin at a stationer's in Roxbury where no one knew his face, ruled by his own hand at his own table after Nell was asleep. Into them, night by night, went copies — made from memory, and his memory for paper was the station's legend — of rosters, desk rotations, beat assignments, the pressroom's sign-out slips; the architecture of his own force, rebuilt in secret in a widower's parlor, so that it could be read the way he had taught two generations to read: cold, owing nothing to anyone in it.

Byrne he cleared first, and the record sets out the method because the method is about to matter.

The desk sergeant had taken Moore's complaint; the desk sergeant kept the book the page was cut from; suspicion owed him its first call. Mercer audited the man's nights the only way left to him — from outside. Four evenings he stood in the rain-shadow of a doorway on Byrne's street and watched the sergeant's windows like a thief: the lamp lit at six, the shadow of the big head against the blind, the parade of six children put to bed in relays, the lamp out at ten, and once, at one in the morning of a night Mercer had marked as unaccounted, the whole house woken glowing by a child's croup, the sergeant's unmistakable bulk walking the floor with a small shape at his shoulder until near dawn.

Byrne came off the page. A man cannot minister to a croup and a murder in the same hours.

But mark — the record requires it marked — what Mercer had just done, and how easily, and with what skill. He had stood in a dark doorway, still, patient, hours together, watching a lodging for its lamp; and no soul who passed him gave the tall grave figure a second look, because he stood like a man whose standing there was proper; and when the lamp went out he walked home through the fog-wet streets and let himself in without a sound, boots dark to the welt, and slept the sleep of a man whose night had answered.

It did not once occur to him that he had just performed, to the letter, the thing described at the rail by Bridget Moore.

Auditors do not audit themselves.

There is no entry in the almanac for that thought, because the thought was never had. The record supplies it here, where it belongs in time, and moves on, as he moved on — to the rosters, and the terrible arithmetic of the four nights.


Chapter Nineteen

The four nights were the twenty-sixth of February, the twenty-ninth of March, the twenty-eighth of April, and the twenty-first of June — the eves and small hours on which four women had been attended to death — and against them Mercer laid the whole muster of the Hanover Street station, name by name, in the Roxbury ledgers, in the quiet hours.

The uniformed force fell away in ranks. Beat men walking posts have sergeants and lamplighters and night bakers for witnesses; the night rotations accounted for forty-one men entire. Day men with families sworn to their suppers and beds accounted for most of the rest — not proof, as Mercer ruled in the margin, but weight. The rail's own night keepers were logged in the station book itself, initialed hour by hour.

When the ledger had done its winnowing there were left over — unaccounted, off duty, unwitnessed, on all four nights — five names.

Two went quickly. A stableman of sixty with a ruptured knee that could not have knelt to the work; a probationary constable of nineteen, boarding with a minister whose household prayed at the very hours in question, loudly, with the lodger required present.

Three names remained, and the record gives them as the ledger gave them, in the order of the ruling:

Sgt. M. Kelleher. Mr. T. Whitmore, records clerk. Det. J. O'Rourke.

Kelleher was a drinker whose nights were unwitnessed because they were spent insensible in the back room of a beer cellar on Endicott Street — established by Mercer in two evenings of the doorway work, the cellar's keeper being voluble and the sergeant being, at one in the morning, a fixture you could have hung a coat on. Not proof. Weight. Mercer ruled him doubtful and moved down the page.

Whitmore, the records clerk, was a smallish silent man of forty who had keys to everything paper in the building, the complaint book included, and hands — Mercer caught himself studying them across the pressroom, and was ashamed, and studied them — narrow as a woman's. For a week Whitmore was the name Mercer's chest tightened at. Then the doorway work found him too: the clerk's unwitnessed nights were spent at a house on North Margin Street whose nature explained both the silence and the lie he'd told the roster about lodging with a cousin; the woman there gave his Februarys and Aprils to Mercer — for a consideration, and with a tariff of contempt thrown in free — evening by remembered evening. Not clean, as ruling goes. But the arithmetic of her ledger — she kept one; everyone in this book keeps one — crossed three of the four nights beyond reasonable purchase.

Ruled: doubtful. Moved down the page.

At the foot of the page, alone under the ruled line, in the Roxbury ledger, in the fair strong hand, stood the last name, and Mercer sat before it in his parlor at two in the morning of the third of July with his palms flat on the table, the way a man sits before a thing he has been outrunning at great cost for a long time.

Det. J. O'Rourke.

Off duty all four nights. Unwitnessed all four nights — lodgings on Salem Street, landlady abed by nine, a private stair to the street, and Salem Street, the ledger noted in its cold margin, lies four minutes' walk from Paull's Court, seven from Hanover's alley. Present rumpled and unsummoned at the Whitfield scene, from the wrong direction, at seven in the morning. Keeper, one week in four, of the rail desk — and the taker, in his own fair round hand, of the Doran complaint, and the Ferris complaint, and the Whitfield complaint, the three filed women dead within weeks of filing, their routes in his writing, their fears in his keeping, their trust —

— the record stops the sentence. The reader has been building it for three hundred pages and may finish it alone.

What the reader cannot know, and shall now, is what it cost the man at the table.

The almanac entry for the third of July is the longest in the book, and much of it belongs to Part Four; but its opening lines belong here, because they are the truest map extant of the border country between Edward Mercer's greatness and his fracture, and the record has promised to show both without favor:

The page has ruled and I will not unrule it. It says the boy. Every method I own, applied without fear or favor, says the boy — and I taught him the methods, which means I must now sit at this table and consider, as a matter of evidence, whether I taught the rest of it too; whether some man can have sat at my elbow four years, taking down my own grammar of patience, and turned it to this; whether the fair hand at my rail and the third place at my table and the son I — write it — the son I had begun to keep against the cold was all of it costume.

I find I would rather it were anyone. I find I would rather it were myself.

Let the reader mark the last sentence, as the pencil marked it — the circle is there, at the line's end, small and deep, the clerk's sign for a sum that would not come out even.

He wrote it as a manner of speaking.

The pencil, as always in this record, was ahead of the man.


Chapter Twenty

The beat ledger broke the last of his resistance, and it broke it with eleven words spoken four months earlier in an April alley.

"Prince Street. I walked that beat for half a winter once."

"Which year?"

"Eighty-one. Or two."

He had them in his scene-book, verbatim, because he kept everything verbatim; and on the fifth of July, in the pressroom, alone, with the station's beat ledgers for the years past open under the lamp, Edward Mercer ran his finger down the winter columns and found what the reader — the record concedes the reader has long been ahead of him here, and observes only that the reader was never the boy's teacher — what the reader has been waiting for him to find.

Prince Street beat, night rotation: Ptn. J. O'Rourke, November 1882 to February 1883.

Not eighty-one. Not "or two." The winter immediately past. The winter Margaret Doran, walking Prince Street home from the mill, had first felt eyes on her back. A man does not misplace last winter. A man files last winter where he files yesterday. He had stood over the dead girl on the lighted way of his own last winter's beat and moved the date casually backward out of reach — the smallest of lies, the kind that never expects a ledger, and there is no reader of hard books who needs telling what a small unnecessary lie sits beside in a column of true entries.

And beneath it, in the pressroom's own sign-out slips, the second thing, the thing that dressed the first in its full clothes: the cold-case files of 1879 — Mercer's four fine books, the seated women, the stopped cluster — signed out to Ptn. J. O'Rourke on eleven occasions across two years of night duty. Eleven. He had said it himself, easily, on the stairs in January — I used to read them. Yours especially. It's how I learned what a file ought to look like — and it had warmed Mercer to hear it, God forgive him, warmed him; and now the sentence stood in the sign-out column eleven entries deep and read entirely otherwise: a young man alone in a pressroom, night upon night, two winters together, studying the anatomy of unsolved stranglings the way — the way a student studies, said one voice in Mercer; and the other voice, the one that ruled pages, said: or the way an apprentice studies. Before his first piece of work, a craftsman reads the masters.

The reader now holds everything. The record pauses to say so plainly, because the reader's arc was asked of it and shall be honored: this is the summit. Off duty the four nights; unwitnessed; lodged four minutes from the last death; first at the third scene, rumpled, from the wrong direction; taker of the three complaints; walker of the girls to the steps; keeper of the routes; the beat on the first street the winter the first woman was marked; the lie about the date; two years' secret study of the pattern's own scripture; young, strong, charming, kind — kind — and the kindness itself now readable as the very instrument, the thing that made the frightened walk toward him easy, at dusk, in the open, gloves on.

Every stone in the arch is true. The record has not cheated one of them. A jury of the reader's peers would vote before supper.

Hold the arch. Feel its weight, how finely it is jointed, how each stone was quarried honestly and laid without malice by the most careful man in Boston.

Now watch the one stone he trimmed to make it close.

That same night — it is in the Roxbury ledger, ruled, dated — Mercer wrote out, as method required, the case against the case. Contra, the column is headed. It is short. Salem St. is near Paull's Ct. — but half the force lodges in the parish. First at the scene — but zeal explains it, and always has. The complaints — but the rotation is public, posted, any hand could time a rail-visit to his desk, and Moore's complaint he did not take. And then the last entry in the Contra column, the stone that would not fit, the one piece of paper in the whole edifice that answered and answered and answered wrong:

Hollis: the grip narrow, clerkly, spare. The boy sculls. I have watched his hands four years — an oarsman's hands, broad in the palm as a plank. They are not the hands in H.'s note.

That is the entry. The record certifies it: his own eye, his own knowledge, his own surgeon's paper, standing square across the road to O'Rourke — the single physical fact in the case, against a summer of circumstance. And beneath it, that night, the master of method wrote the sentence by which the method died, and the record gives it as written, for it is the true fracture line of this book, the place where the reader's foundation goes — not at the wharf, not at any reveal, but here, in a tired man's parlor, in fair strong handwriting, in the space of eleven words:

But a grip may be trained narrow, as a hand is gloved.

It may not. He knew it may not. Hollis had said spare bones, said clerkly, said built so — and Edward Mercer, who had never in twenty-nine years bent a fact, took the one exonerating stone in the arch and trimmed it with a sentence, because the arch had to close, because if the arch did not close on the boy it stood open over something else — over salt-white boots and thin nights and a figure in yard-mouths that stood the way furniture stands — and no man alive trims a true stone knowing why. The knowing is exactly what the trimming is for.

The almanac, that night, has no words at all.

Only a circle, pressed through the page.


Chapter Twenty-One

Nell broke the last of it, at Sunday supper, the thirteenth of July, with the pot in her hand and no idea to her dying day — the record must hope — of what she set moving.

The third place was empty that week; O'Rourke had sent his regrets, casework; the two of them ate alone in the long evening light, and Nell, making conversation against her father's silence, which had grown all summer like the shadow in a consumption, said the thing lightly, fondly, in praise:

"John's been good to me, these months, you know — you needn't ever worry over my school evenings. Since the spring, whenever the settlement classes run past dark, he contrives to be up Parmenter Street on some errand and walks me to our corner. Rain or fine. I've told him it's foolishness, a great girl like me, but he only laughs and — Father, it's the sweetest thing — he never leaves at the corner. He waits, at the bottom of the hill, till I'm up it and through the door. I've watched him from the window. He waits till the door shuts, and then he goes. Every time. He waits till I'm through the door."

The clock walked through the silence.

"Since the spring," Mercer said. His voice, Nell said afterward — she said it to only one person, and long after, and it is how the record has it — his voice was wrong the way a floor is wrong when water has got into it. Level to the eye. Dead underfoot.

"April, may be? Since the Whitfield girl, I suppose, or near it. Father, what — "

"Which evenings, Nell. Think. Which evenings does the school run late."

"Wednesdays. And the sewing class Saturdays, since May. Father, you're white as the cloth — "

Wednesdays and Saturdays. He was seeing the Roxbury ledger, the columns only he could see: the girls had died on a Saturday, a Saturday, a Sunday's small hours, a Sunday's small hours. He was seeing dusk on Parmenter Street, the settlement lamps coming on, the young man contriving to be there, rain or fine, patient as an archway; the walking-courtesy, the very courtesy, the steps and the lighted way; his daughter, twenty-two, a young woman of the parish who walked alone at dark — the exact description, said the ledger voice, coldly, of the four — walked home easy, at dusk, in the open, trusting, by a man who then stood, still, at the bottom of the hill, watching her window for the lamp, waiting on the light, and went when the door was shut —

— he waits till I'm through the door —

and Edward Mercer stood up from his own table so fast the chairs cried out, and stood with his knuckles on the cloth in the long light, a tall grey man swaying very slightly, while his daughter said his name with the pot still in her hand; and the record, which has now brought the reader to the exact center of the maze, to the room where every path of this book crosses, will state without decoration what was standing in that room, because the reader must hold both of its truths at once to go a step further:

A father who believed, on a summer's weight of honest evidence, that the thing he had hunted all year had been walking his daughter home twice a week and watching her window for the light.

And a daughter who was in no danger on those evenings, and had never been — who was walked and warded, rain or fine, by a young man who could not have said fully why he did it; who knew only that his teacher's nights could not be accounted for; that the old cluster and the new had one constant and the constant poured his wine on Sundays; that he could prove nothing, and would sooner have died than say it, and so did the one thing a man can do with a fear he cannot file:

He put himself between it and what it might love next.

Two guardians. Each certain where the danger stood. Each standing watch against the other in the dark, all summer, over the same lit window.

The record knows of no sentence it could add to that.

The chapter is over. The arch is closed. The reader knows everything.

The reader knows nothing.



Chapter Twenty-Two

He shadowed the boy for eleven nights, and the record of those nights is the strangest passage in the Roxbury ledgers, because it is a great detective's flawless surveillance of an innocent man, annotated throughout by a hunted thing wearing the detective's hand.

The method was the doorway method, now perfected on Byrne and Kelleher and the records clerk; and no man in Boston — the record says it without irony, weighing every word — no man in Boston was better fitted for it. He could stand in a yard-mouth from ten until two with the stillness of furniture. He knew how to wait on a lamp. He knew which shadows a street lends its watchers and at which hours it takes them back; he had, though the sentence must wait a few chapters to be understood in full, five years of nights' practice he could not remember having.

What the eleven nights yielded, item by item:

That John O'Rourke lived as he was rostered, worked as he was paid, and walked of an evening more than any young man in Boston with a bed. Wednesdays and Saturdays, Parmenter Street, the settlement lamps, Nell to the corner, the wait at the hill's foot — Mercer watched his daughter's window make its yellow square and watched the boy below watch it, twenty minutes together on the worst night, motionless in fog you could have wrung out like a cloth, and stood forty feet behind him in a yard-mouth learning what it looks like, a patient shape waiting on a lit window, and did not once — the reader is asked to believe it, and it is true — did not once hear the yard-mouth say what it had to say to him. The lesson stood forty feet ahead of him wearing the wrong face. Lessons generally do.

That on the Tuesday the boy sculled on the Charles at dawn, and Mercer, from the boathouse shadow with a glass, watched the oar handled and the boat lifted ashore one-armed, and saw with a clarity the glass made cruel the hands — broad as planks, knuckled like rope — and stood a long minute with Hollis's note reciting itself in him, narrow, spare, clerkly, and wrote that evening in the Contra column one word, underlined: hands; and under it, the sentence already ruled there a week before, a grip may be trained narrow — and the record observes, for the last time and then never again, that he had now seen the exonerating fact through a surveyor's glass in good light, and traded it away a second time, at a worse rate.

And that on the ninth night, the boy's late walk did not end at his Salem Street stair.

It ended at Mercer's own street.

The record must be exact here, for the scene is the book's hinge and both its readings hang on the exactness. Eleven o'clock, the fog coming up. O'Rourke came down the hill by the far walk, easy, unhurried, hat low — and stopped opposite the Mercer house, in the gap between gaslamps where the railings make a pool of black, and stood. Still. A quarter of an hour. Twenty minutes. The house dark but for the parlor lamp, which was Mercer's lamp, lit against his supposed presence within — and the watcher across the street watched it, motionless, the way furniture stands, while forty yards up the hill, in the mouth of the mews, the man who owned the lamp watched the watcher, each of them fixed on the same yellow square, the fog thickening between and around and over them both, until the parlor lamp — Nell, retiring, on the clockwork of her habits — went out.

The watcher across the street stayed a further five minutes on the dead dark house.

Then he settled his hat and walked home to Salem Street, pausing twice to look back, and let himself in by the private stair; and his light burned behind his blind another hour; and Edward Mercer stood in the mews-mouth in the wringing fog long after it died, arriving over and over, like a man rehearsing a fall, at the only sentence his summer had left him the grammar to build:

He watches the house when he believes I am in it. He is not watching over. He is watching for. My lamp. My hours. My household.

I am not hunting him at all. He is hunting me — through her —

and the true sentence, the one the fog had stood there whole nights trying to hand him — he watches your house because he fears for it; he is hunting the same thing you are; you have been standing guard against each other since April — passed through the space between the two men, unclaimed, the way truth mostly moves through this record: present, patient, wearing no light of its own, waiting to be filed by whoever will take it.

Nobody took it.

The fog closed the street behind them both.


Chapter Twenty-Three

He moved on his household within the week, and to move on his household he had to lie to it, and the record has said the man did not lie, so the record must now watch him learn.

Nell was sent to Worcester on the last Sunday of July — her mother's sister, a strong invitation arranged by two posts and a fiction: the aunt's health, a companion wanted for August, the change to do Nell good after her hard nursing spring. Nell fought it as a daughter fights, which is to say with three true sentences at the breakfast table — "You are sending me from the house. You look like death and you are sending away the one soul who watches you eat. What is it you mean to do in August, Father, that I mayn't see?" — and her father, grey and steady, gave her back the whole force of twenty-nine unimpeachable years, and the one lie of his life he never entered in any book:

"There is a man to be taken, and until he is taken I'll work the nights, and I can work them better with your window dark."

True. Every word — the record certifies it bitterly — every word true, and its assembled meaning as false as any sentence sworn in any dock: for she took him to mean I fear for you, girl, and what he meant — what he had ruled and re-ruled in Roxbury ink until it looked like proof — was your window is the bait he rises to; your lamp is his hour; I am clearing the trap-ground.

She went on the Tuesday. He carried her box to the depot himself, and on the platform she did the thing that nearly broke the summer's whole machinery in one stroke: held his coat front in both fists, looked up at him the way her mother used to when the ruled world wanted defying, and said —

"Whatever it is you can't say. You'll come back from it. Say that, and I'll board."

"I'll come back from it," said Edward Mercer.

The record notes it as the second lie, tendered eleven days after the first; and notes that he watched the train to the curve; and notes what the platform's other lie would have been, had anyone asked the daughter hers — for she had written, two nights prior, unbeknown, a small note in her clear school-teaching hand, and posted it to a Salem Street lodging:

"Mr. O'Rourke — I am packed off to Worcester for August and can't think how to refuse. I know you and Father are at some work you neither of you name. I ask you what I cannot ask him, because he is the case and you are his friend: watch him. He does not sleep, he does not eat, he stands at the parlor window half the night looking at the street as if it owed him an answer. Whatever hunts this parish, it is eating my father first. — N.M."

The letter reached its stair on the thirtieth of July.

The reader now holds every string of the last machine, and the record, before it throws the lever, will count them once, aloud, because no chapter after this one will pause for counting:

A detective certain his protégé is the killer, baiting a trap with his own house.

A protégé certain — no; not certain; sick with a thing he will not let become certainty — that his teacher is the constant in two clusters of dead women, now formally begged by the teacher's own daughter to watch the man through August's nights.

An empty house. A dark window. Two patient men, each trained by the same master in the same method, converging on the same lamp — each believing himself the guard, each appearing in the other's careful ledger as the thing to be taken.

And below them both, punctual as tide, rising toward its August, the third party to the summer — unrostered, unwitnessed, wearing the best-trusted face in Boston, keeping its own fair-handed books — for whom no trap had been laid at all, because neither of its two hunters had ever once seen it plain: the one man in the city that both their ledgers had cleared.

The quiet hours were coming.

They kept, as always, their own calendar.


Chapter Twenty-Four

The station took him off the case on the fourth of August, and the scene has been told in the building's folklore so many ways that the record is glad to hold the true one.

There was no shouting. There was a chair placed, and Deven not behind his desk but in front of it, hip on the wood, which twenty years of subordinates knew for the posture of bad news among friends, and there was the captain's voice with all the committee-tone gone out of it.

"Byrne found you at the rail book at four this morning. Thursday it was the pressroom, and you couldn't give him the day of the week. You stood Kate Flynn's Mondays till she asked was I working you to death — Kate Flynn, Mercer, asked after your health. I have a detective who hasn't slept since June, walks the parish nights like a soul in purgatory, keeps case papers in his house — no, I don't know what's in the Roxbury drawer, and I don't want to; I know there is one; I'm a policeman too, whatever this office has made of me." He rubbed his face with both hands, once, hard. "You are the best I ever had or heard of. And you are done, Edward. A man burning down isn't a lamp. As of today you're on rest, by order, full pay, and I'll have your active files on Sullivan's desk by six."

"You'll have paper on his desk by six." Mercer's voice was, all witnesses agree, entirely level. "The case is past paper, Captain. It's one man's now — it can only be finished by the man it's — " and there the level voice stopped itself, one word from the cliff, and Deven, watching his old friend's face do something small and terrible around the unsaid word, mistook it — as everyone in this record mistakes everything, on schedule, forgivably, fatally — for grief and overwork.

"Go home," Deven said gently. "That's the whole order. Sleep, man. Whatever it is — it will keep a fortnight."

Mercer stood, took up his hat, and at the door turned, and the record preserves the exchange because it is the last time the two men ever spoke as colleagues.

"You told me once the trust is the whole cathedral."

"I did, and it is."

"I've one question of it, then, and you may charge the asking to my fever." He set the hat on, quite steady. "When it turns out the beast has been sheltering in the cathedral all along, wearing the vestments, keeping the ledgers, ringing the changes — is the trust still the thing, Captain? Or is the trust the beast's own church, and every soul that ever taught this parish to stop looking at the coat — " a pause, no longer than a page-turn — "an unpaid sexton of it?"

He did not wait for the answer.

There was none in the room to wait for.


Chapter Twenty-Five

What he did with the first week of enforced rest, the almanac and the Roxbury ledgers record in a hand that grows small and crowded, like a man lowering his voice.

He slept — write it plainly — almost not at all. He perfected the trap on paper; the reader shall see it built in the next chapter. He walked the four scenes in daylight, measuring nothing new, an act the record can only call devotional. And on the ninth of August, a Saturday, at some hour he could not afterward supply, Edward Mercer went to Copp's Hill burying ground.

He had not decided to go. That sentence is the chapter, and everything else in it is only the sentence walking.

The record has it from the almanac entire, and gives it so, because paraphrase would be a kind of lie:

Came to myself at C— Hill in the last light, at the northeast wall. Hat in hand. Flowers in the hat — white stock, fresh, a bunch bought that day. I do not remember buying flowers. I do not remember the walk. The last whole hour I can produce is four o'clock, the Roxbury table, ruling the trap's second page — then the wall, the light going, the stock in my hat.

I was not at Abigail's stone. I know her stone as I know my own hand. I was standing at the northeast wall where the ground slopes, three graves along from the corner — and I knew the grave I stood at, that is the matter, I knew it before I read it, the way a man knows his own street at night by the feel of the ground. Meehan. The servant girl. November of '79.

The stock was already laid. Not dropped — laid, squared at the stone's base, the stems evened. Clerk's work.

I walked the row then, because I am what I am even in the ruin of it. Kelly, the hawker's daughter, four stones west: at her base, gone brown, a fortnight old or near it, a like bunch. White stock. Squared. And at the widow Cross's, the March grave, older again, the stems only, tied, the heads long taken by weather.

Three visits. A season of them. Standing evidence, in my own method's terms — the attendance is regular, the arrangement is careful, the hand returns and returns — of a mourner who has kept these three graves faithfully, quietly, in the evening hours, for God knows how long.

I have stood in this ground before at day's end with flowers I do not remember buying. The sentence is now written. It has been true for years and it is written at last. Somebody keeps my dead. Somebody in this city keeps the '79 women as I keep Abigail — and he buys white stock, and squares the stems, and comes in the thin hours, and today I surfaced from wherever I go standing in his exact footprint with his exact flowers in my hat.

The reader is watching a man hold a lamp at arm's length from the last door in the corridor. The record will not reach past him to open it. It sets down only, in fairness to everything that follows, the entry's final ruled lines, the sound of the door being held shut by main strength in a fair strong hand:

There is a mind at work that has studied me so long and so close it can dress itself in my habits — my hours, my stillness, my flowers, my grief. It stands where I would stand. It grieves as I grieve. It has made of me its pattern-book, and I know of one man only who has had four years' apprenticeship in all I am.

The boy has learned to walk in my very steps.

That must be what this is.

That must be what this is.

Written twice. The second time, the almanac notes, the pencil went through the page at the full stop; and there is no circle at that date's corner, for the first time since March; because a circle is a clerk's mark for a sum that will not come out even, and this sum had come out — had been made to come out — and the reader of ledgers knows what it is called when a sum is made to come out.

It is called a false balance.

It is the only one in Edward Mercer's books.

He carried it home through the dark like a man carrying a full glass over cobbles, and did not spill it, and set it on the Roxbury table, and finished ruling the trap.


Chapter Twenty-Six

The trap was made of paper, because paper was — the reader has heard the sentence; the reader may now hear what it had always meant — paper was the man's element.

Its mechanism was the day-book: the station's running log of case-notes and memoranda, kept in the detectives' room, read religiously by every man of the plainclothes strength and by no soul outside the building. A fact planted there was a fish placed in one pool. Only station men drank at it.

On the eleventh of August, Mercer — on rest, but no order had ever kept a senior man's hand from the day-book, and Sullivan, holding his files, was glad of any note from the great man — entered, in the fair strong hand, a memorandum:

Doran matter, addendum. Have located a witness overlooked in Feb. — the lamplighter whose Prince St. round passes the passage-mouth, on his ladder before light the morning after. States he saw a man leave the passage and pass directly under his lamp; can give the face. Has been abroad to a sister in Halifax since March, wherefore missed. Returns by the Cunard boat Saturday next. I will take his statement and bring him to view the muster Monday morning. — E.M.

Every word false. The first wholly false document of Edward Mercer's life, composed with the care he had given true ones for twenty-nine years, and reading — Sullivan said it admiringly at the inquiry, not knowing what he said — reading exactly like Mercer.

The geometry was this. No lamplighter existed; no boat; no Monday muster. But to the one reader in the building for whom that memorandum was a sentence of death, it named a place, and it started a clock. The witness who could give the face would land Saturday; would be seen Monday; and between the entry's reading and the boat's landing there ran five nights in which a guilty man — a man trained, as the memorandum's author had trained him, never to leave a wrong figure standing in a book — must go to Prince Street in the dark hours and learn the lamplighter's round for himself: which lamp, what ladder, what line of sight from the passage-mouth; must stand in the passage where the thing was done and look up at the light and measure his own ruin. He would not send. He would not wait. He would go, and stand, and Mercer — nine nights' perfect in the doorway craft, invisible in his own parish, patient as the arch — would be in the yard-mouth opposite, watching the trap's lamp the way all the traps in this book watch lamps.

And whoever came and stood in that passage in the small hours, measuring — whoever the fish was that rose in the pool only station men drank from —

then it is the boy, says the Roxbury ledger, in the last ruled line of its last worked page, and the false balance is true, and I will take him at the scene of his study, and it ends.

The ledger does not rule the other line. The record rules it now, where it has belonged since a Tuesday in January when a mill girl twisted her gloves at a rail:

And if it is not the boy — if the pool's other drinker rises — if the figure that comes to stand in Prince Street passage in the thin hours, still as furniture, measuring the lamp, is taller, and greyer, and comes by no stair anyone watches, and stands in its own exact footprint —

who then is waiting in the yard-mouth to take him?

The bell of the Old North rang the changes over the parish, unhurried, iron on iron.

The quiet hours kept their calendar.

Saturday came.


END OF PART THREE



PART FOUR — QUIET HOURS


Chapter Twenty-Seven

The Cunard boat was due Saturday, said the day-book, and so the trap's five nights ran Monday to Friday, and Mercer stood them all.

He had provisioned like a campaigner: cold meat and bread in his pockets, the dark lantern he never lit, the grey duster coat that made him a piece of any wall he leaned on. By eight each evening he was in the yard-mouth opposite the Prince Street passage, in the pooled black between the gaslamps, and there he stayed until the sky went grey, still as the arch, watching the lamp above the passage-mouth — the lamplighter's lamp, the fiction's anchor, the one point of light in his own lie — with a patience that had stopped being effort years before and become, though he was the last man in Boston who could afford to know it, his nature.

The record has his vigil-notes, pencilled small against his knee in the dark, and they are the notes of the best surveillance man of his age: the passage's night traffic timed and charactered — the oyster-shop boy's shortcut at ten, the dock pair courting at eleven, gone by half past, the Angelus drunk who slept Thursdays in the third doorway; every figure entered, weighed, cleared. Four nights, nothing rose to the bait. He recorded that too, without impatience: Nothing. Good. A fish that waits is a fish that read the boat's day right. He will come Friday, late, when one night's risk buys certainty. I would.

I would. The record lets the two words stand unexamined, as he did.

What his vigil-notes do not hold — what nothing held until the inquiry pieced the week together from a boathouse keeper, a coffee-stall man, and a certain landlady's private stair — is the vigil's second watcher.

John O'Rourke had read the memorandum on the Monday, in the day-book, at Sullivan's elbow, and had known it for false before he reached its signature.

Not suspected. Known. And the manner of the knowing must be given now, for it is the turn of the whole machine: the young man had spent two winters of night duty reading the 1879 casebooks — eleven sign-outs; the record showed them long ago — and he had read them as only the obsessed read, to the ink, to the margins; and in the second book of four, at the winter's desperate end, there is a memorandum in the fair strong hand announcing a witness overlooked: a lamplighter, abroad since the crimes, returning by boat, who saw a man plain beneath his lamp and can give the face.

There was no lamplighter in 1879 either.

The '79 trap ran nine nights, said the old books, and took nothing, and its failure is annotated in a single line that a young reader on the Prince Street beat had once copied into his own pocket-book because it frightened him and he could not say why:

No fish rose. Query: is he not of the pool after all — or does he somehow know the bait from above it?

Does he somehow know the bait from above it. A killer who could read the trap through the trap-setter's own eyes. The sentence had sat in John O'Rourke's pocket-book for two years, and in his chest all that summer beside a daughter's letter — watch him — and when the same memorandum rose again in the same hand baiting the same street, the student did the sum the reader has watched him refuse all year, and this time did not refuse it: either the fish of '83 has read the file of '79 — or the two traps have one author, and the author is the pool, and the bait cannot catch him because a man cannot rise to himself.

There remained one test, and it was terrible in its simplicity. The trap named a place and five nights. Watch the place. Watch all five. If the teacher stands vigil in the yard-mouth, night upon night, hunting — then he is the hunter, and somewhere a real fish may yet rise, and two watchers are better than one.

But if on some thin hour the yard-mouth stands empty — if the grey coat is gone from its post and a figure is in the passage instead, under the fiction's lamp, still, measuring, standing in the exact print of a thing that was never there —

then God help them both.

So the five nights of Prince Street held, unknown to each other, the two best-trained watchers in Boston: the master in the yard-mouth watching the passage, and forty feet above him, behind the parapet of the cooperage roof, reached by a yard ladder before every dusk, the student — watching the passage, and the yard-mouth, and the master, all three; sleepless, silent, sick at heart; the only soul in the city, that week, with his eyes on the true pool.

Four nights, two watchers, nothing.

Then Friday came, and with it the fog and the rain together, off the harbor in slow grey freights; and the record must now do the hardest of all its offices, and go down into the passage with the reader for the last time.


Chapter Twenty-Eight

The rain moved through the lamplight in slow waves, like the sound of a bell.

Mercer took his post at eight. The vigil-notes for Friday open in the usual small firm pencil — Fog thick, rain steady. Traffic nil after ten. Lamp burning well. — and the reader who turns that page in the evidence box must be told what every man at the inquiry needed telling, because the change is otherwise past belief.

The next entry is timed half past eleven, and the hand has begun to slope.

The entry after has no time at all, and the words run:

bell somewhere. iron. unhurried

And there are no entries after that.

What the vigil-notes ceased to see, the cooperage roof saw whole, and the record gives the rest of the night in the only voice that watched it through: the testimony, later sworn, signed, and sealed into this file, of Detective John O'Rourke — begged by him to be taken down entire, though it broke him to give it, because his teacher had taught him that paper answers, and this was the last page on which he could pay the debt.

"At about half past eleven I observed Detective Mercer leave the yard-mouth.

"I must say it exactly. I had watched him four nights. A man on watch shifts, eases a knee, minds his warmth — even he, even Mercer, a little, humanly, once an hour. What left the yard-mouth at half past eleven did not move like that, nor like a man abandoning a post. It moved the way — I have tried for the words all these weeks. It moved the way a thing moves that has never considered being watched. It had no wariness in it anywhere. It crossed the open street in the rain, slowly, directly, indifferent to the lamps, and it was his height and his coat and it was not — God forgive me — it was not anyone. It was a stillness, walking.

"It entered the passage and stopped beneath the lamp, where the fiction had put the lamplighter's ladder, and it stood.

"I will be asked how long. Forty minutes by my watch. Forty minutes in the standing rain, without motion of any kind, the head a little lifted toward the lamp. I have stood post in rain; there is no man breathing stands forty minutes so. I knew by the second minute that I was not watching a vigil. I was watching an attendance. The word is his. It is the right word. Whatever stood under that lamp was keeping an appointment that the rest of us had only ever read the minutes of.

"And I must swear to the next in the order it happened, though no order can make it bearable.

"I came down the ladder. I entered the passage mouth. The rain covered my noise, or nothing could have; I do not believe, now, that noise was ever the question. And from three yards, with my lantern shut and my heart going like a scaffold drum, I said the sentence I had built on that roof over four nights for whoever should finally stand beneath that lamp.

"I said: 'There's no lamplighter, John.'

"I had prepared it for a young man. My own name, sir — you will ask why. Because I had prayed all week it would be some other John, any John, half the parish is John — and because if it was a fish I did not know, the name would strike as nonsense and hold him a half-second in confusion, and a half-second is an arrest. That was the theory of it. I offer it so the court may see we were his students to the last, all of us, in everything.

"The figure did not startle. Nothing so quick as startle. It came about slowly, the whole frame together, the way a door comes about on its hinge, and the lamp got up into the face under the hat-brim, and I saw that I had known for four nights and had been calling the knowledge by other names to keep my feet.

"It was my teacher's face. And it was at rest.

"I have seen Edward Mercer's face at every duty and every grief. Working, it is a lit room. Mourning, it is a locked one. This was neither. This was the face of a man in the deepest and easiest sleep of his life, with the eyes open, and the rain running on it unregarded; and it looked at me — through me — at the space I stood in; and the lips moved, and what they said, in a voice I knew and did not, quiet, courteous, unhurried, was:

"'You'll freeze, miss.'

"Gentlemen, I am sworn, and I will finish.

"I said his name — his right name, Edward, which I had never in my life presumed to use — and at the third saying of it the rain seemed to reach him, and something came up through the stillness the way a drowned thing comes up through water, slow, from a great depth; and the face — I have no craft for this; I will say it plain and be done. The face arrived. It arrived into itself, feature by feature, the way I once watched him arrive at Copp's Hill over his own laid flowers. And Edward Mercer stood under the lamp in the rain, with his vigil-notes soaked in his pocket and forty minutes gone from the world, and looked at me, and looked at the passage around him, and at the lamp above him — and I watched the best detective God ever made read his own scene.

"He did not speak for a long time.

"Then he said, in his own voice, the levellest I ever heard it: 'How long have I stood here.'

"'Forty minutes, sir.'

"'And before tonight.' Not a question. He was ruling columns. 'The other nights of the week. You've had the roof — no, don't answer, the ladder's been moved fresh each dusk, I marked it Tuesday and cleared you as a nesting pigeon. Attend to the question, John, and answer it as I taught you, whatever it does to either of us. The other nights. Did I hold my post?'

"'Every hour of them, sir. Monday to Thursday you never left the yard-mouth.'

"'And in seventy-nine,' said Edward Mercer, under the lamp, in the rain, 'there were nine such nights, and no one on the roof at all.'

"And he took out his pocket-book, and pencilled one line standing — I saw the page after; every man of the inquiry has seen it; it is the evidence upon which all the rest of this file stands or nothing does — and closed the book, and said to me, courteous as a man asking the time: 'You'll accompany me home, Detective. Your arm, if you'll lend it — I find I don't presently trust my own navigation. There is a drawer in my house that you and I are going to open, and a great many pages that are going to answer at last, and we have until Monday's muster to do what must be done in the correct order.'

"The line in the pocket-book reads:

"Query answered. He knew the bait from above it. — E.M."


Chapter Twenty-Nine

They walked out of Prince Street arm in arm, the teacher and the student, two figures in the emptying rain, and any soul abroad at that hour would have seen a young officer helping an old one home, and would have thought nothing, and would at last — the record notes it without bitterness, being past bitterness — have been right.

The house was dark. Nell's window dark in Worcester, ninety miles safe. Mercer lit the parlor lamp himself, made up the fire himself, stood a moment with his hands to it; and then, being Mercer, took off his soaked coat, hung it square, and turned to the work.

"The Roxbury drawer you know of. Deven knows of it; half the station guesses. Case papers." He was crossing to the study as he spoke, walking carefully, a man on his own stairs after an illness. "It has a false bottom. You'll not have known that. I had it joined in 'seventy-nine — I could not have told you why I wanted a hidden ledger-space, John; I remember choosing the joiner for his closeness of mouth and thinking, as I paid him, that a detective ought to keep a place even his own methods couldn't find. I invite you to weigh that sentence as evidence. I have been weighing it for an hour."

The drawer came out; the papers Deven guessed at came out — rosters, the Roxbury books, the summer's whole architecture; and Mercer felt along the joinery, and pressed, and lifted the second floor of the thing away.

The record inventories what lay beneath, exactly, as the inquiry inventoried it, because the reader has earned the plain list and the plain list is past all decorating:

Item. A ledger, marbled boards, unlabelled. Within, in the fair strong hand throughout — the hand of every exhibit in this file, the hand of the vigil-notes and the almanac and the memorandum and this record itself — pages ruled in columns headed NAME · STREET · HOURS · LAMP. Nine women's names. Their routes, set down finer than any beat-book: the mill gates and shop doors, the horse-car stops, the walking-friends and their defection days, the lamps-out times observed night upon night, dated, patient, exact. Margaret Doran's pages run from the fourteenth of January. Bridget Moore's from the sixth of June. Three of the nine names are struck through, with a date beside each. A fourth, Moore, likewise. The remaining five names — the record has verified each — are the five spared women of the complaint book, their pages simply stopping, mid-column, as if the reader of them had been called away.

Item. Folded square, pressed flat as by long keeping: a leaf razored from a complaint book, the entry of the fourth of June in Sergeant Byrne's schoolroom hand.

Item. A second marbled ledger, older, its boards water-stained. Three names. The winter of 1878–79. The same columns. The same hand, younger.

Item. Beneath all, wrapped in a woman's handkerchief gone brown at its folds: a hackney-office receipt of June 1878 for a mourning-coach; a hospital subscription card in the name of Abigail Mercer; and a bunch of white stock, pressed and papery with five years, the stems evened, tied with black thread.

The fire ticked in the next room. The rain had stopped.

O'Rourke — his testimony carries the moment, and carries it in six words, and the record has never found better — said: "He read the ledger standing. All of it."

Every page. The nine women. The struck lines and their dates, which were the dates ruled into his own case-books all that spring by his own day-hand, hunting. The five stopped columns — the spared, spared by nothing but the calendar of his surfacing, the day-man's returning tide. The old book, the first winter, the first three, kept through the black year while a station said work was saving him. His wife's mourning-coach. The flowers.

The books were true. They had always been true. Here at the bottom of the last drawer was the proof of the whole faith of his life carried to its end: every fact ruled, every column exact, every entry made fairly by a patient hand in the quiet hours — the finest surveillance record he had ever read, and he had written it, and he had never once been lied to by a ledger, and he was not being lied to now.

He closed the marbled boards at last, and set the book down square with the drawer's edge, and stood looking at his own two hands — narrow, spare, clerkly; the note had been in his book since March — and then Edward Mercer pulled out the chair, and sat, and reached for clean paper and the pen, because there was work, and he was Mercer, and the work was now at last one column wide and one column had always been his best distance.

"Sit down, John," he said. "You'll want ink of your own. We have a great deal to take down, and I am the only witness left worth the name — and I will be examined properly, by the method, every date checked against every book, no weight given to remorse and none to affection, exactly as I taught you.

"Begin with what answers."


Chapter Thirty

They worked from Saturday midnight to Monday's small hours, with the lamp between them, and what they built in those thirty hours the reader has been holding all along.

It is this record.

The reader was promised, at the outset, a plain accounting of who knew what, and when; the reader has perhaps wondered, chapter by chapter, at the record's strange offices — its insistence that nothing in it is false; its patience with ledgers; its way of standing a pace behind a certain grey figure and seeing every soul in Boston plain except him. The record has no more need of the mystery. It was begun on that Saturday night at the Roxbury table, in the fair strong hand, at the dictation of its own subject and by the strength of his own method — every scene walked again, every paper produced and cited, every witness's words given from the verbatim books; the almanac opened and copied entry by entry with its circles and its false balance; the victim's mornings rebuilt from the fugue's own surveillance columns and from what came up, in those thirty hours, out of the thin places — for it came up, at the end, when the day-man stopped holding the door: the fog, the bell, the names said courteously in the dark, the kettle-turnings of trust; not memory as men have memory, he said, but arrivals, like his face in the rain — and John O'Rourke took it all down, and checked every date against every book, and gave no weight to remorse and none to affection, and the two of them together, teacher and student, master and hand, examined Edward Mercer by the full rigor of Edward Mercer, and closed the case.

The reader holds the fair copy.

The confession proper — the legal instrument, sworn, witnessed, its every claim cross-cited to the exhibits — runs eleven pages and is annexed to this record's end, and the record will not rehearse it, save for three of its provisions, which were to Mercer the whole point of the paper and must be to this book.

First: Daniel Flynn. The instrument establishes his innocence past any prosecutor's twisting — the Deer Island certificate annexed, the Moore staging explained under oath by the one hand that did it, the timeline sworn. He is to be released and the city's regret published, said the ledger-voice, dictating, and compensated from my estate, which is adequate, and my daughter provided from what remains, the figures following, exact to the dollar, because he was Mercer to the last line.

Second: John O'Rourke. The instrument states — sworn, initialed, in its author's hand — that the detective's conduct throughout was not merely blameless but exemplary; that he suspected rightly and early and bore it alone; that concealment can attach to no act of his, all having been done inside sixty hours and the whole of it delivered to authority by this instrument; and it commends — the old hand pressing hard here — his fitness for the senior detective's duty in every respect but one, which time will mend: he is still capable of believing that a beloved thing cannot be the thing in the pattern. The books do not share this incapacity. Teach the books.

Third: the manner of proceeding. And here the record must simply set down the argument as it ran across the table in the Sunday dark, because two men said honest things and neither was wholly right, and the reader is owed both.

O'Rourke was for the muster and the dock. "You taught me the whole cathedral, sir — the trust is kept by the trial. It must be public, it must be process, or it's one more page cut from one more book. You above anyone. The parish must see that the coat answers."

"The parish must see that the coat answers," Mercer agreed. "It will see it. It will read every page — the confession is the trial, John; I have cross-examined myself these thirty hours harder than any silk alive could, and you have checked me; there is no fact a courtroom could add. What a courtroom would add is the other thing. The months of it. The girl in Worcester in a witness-box. Kate Flynn made a spectacle beside her brother. Byrne's smile, Verey's window, Loomis — Loomis, John — dragged up again by every sheet in New England, being looked at, being looked at, and you know now what the looking does. And at the end of the months, the same rope, and around it a parish taught for half a year that the man they trusted longest was the beast — taught it slow, taught it as entertainment, until trust itself is the joke of the North End and every coat on every peg is a punchline. That is not the cathedral kept, Detective. That is the beast's last kill, made from the cell.

"There is a shorter accounting. It is in the oldest book of all: the life for the lives. I have taken the statement of the last witness. There remains only the sentence, and I am well studied — " the level voice, for one word's space, was not level — "in how such sentences are carried out quietly, at night, by water, by a patient hand. Let the instrument be found Monday. Let the parish have the truth whole, in one morning, with the account already closed. Grief, John. Not theatre. The dead are owed grief."

The lamp burned. The student looked a long time at his teacher.

"You are asking me to let you go to the harbor."

"I am telling you," Mercer said gently, "what is already ruled. I ask nothing of you but the thing I have asked all year: keep the books. Finish the record when its author cannot. And when they ask you — they will ask you, all your life — whether the great Mercer's method was sound, seeing where it carried him, tell them the truth as you have checked it line by line:

"The method never failed. Not once, boy. The books were true from the first page.

"It was the reader that failed. And the reader has at last — " and he squared the finished pages at the table's edge, and capped the ink, and looked at his student across the lamp with the face O'Rourke swore afterward was, for the first time that year, entirely at rest —

"— the reader has at last caught up."



Chapter Thirty-One

There remain to this record some few pages in the first hand, and the reader shall have them as they stand in the original, for they were written in the hour before light on the Monday, at the cleared table, with the instrument sworn and squared and the ink capped, and they are the last columns Edward Mercer ruled.

To my daughter I have written separately, and that letter is hers and not the record's, saving one line, which I set down here because the record has earned it and she will forgive the sharing. I have written: You asked me on a platform to say I would come back from it, and I said the words, and they were the one lie of my life. I ask you to weigh, against that lie, twenty-nine years of the truth, and to keep the almanac, and to mark in it, if you can bear the keeping, that your father's last entry was made in his right mind, in the quiet hours, with the sum at last come even.

To the parish I have no sentence that the instrument does not say better, in the proper form, with the exhibits annexed.

To the reader — for a record kept as this one has been kept will have readers; it is built for them; it was always, I now understand, built for you — I owe the last accounting, and it is short.

You trusted me. You were right to. That is the whole terror of the thing, and I will not soften it in my final hour: the trust was not misplaced, it was incomplete. The man you walked beside all these pages was true — the method true, the grief true, the hunting true, every ruled line of it. He was simply not the whole of what wore the coat, and he did not know the remainder, and could not have known it, because the remainder kept its books in the one drawer his method never opens.

Every man has such a drawer. Most are empty. Mine was not.

Ask, before you close this book, who audits you, reader. Who has the keys to your pressroom in the hours you cannot account for. And if you answer, as the parish answered all that year, as I answered — there is nothing in my nights, I am tired is all, the days are whole — then you have learned the record's one lesson, and I give it you now with the water already loud in my hearing and no reason left on earth to flatter either of us:

The days are always whole. It is not the days that keep the books.

The bell is ringing the half hour. Iron, unhurried. I have heard it all my life and I know now on whose behalf it was always tolling, and I find the knowledge — this will surprise no one who has read me fairly — I find the knowledge orderly.

My coat is on the peg. The fog is on the harbor. The tide, I have checked it in the almanac, is at the full, and the water off the head of the wharf runs deep and cold and quick, and a patient man who knows how long things take can be exact even in this.

The record passes here to its finisher, who will do it faithfully, being the best of us.

Begin with what answers.

— E.M.


Chapter Thirty-Two

The hand changes here. The reader will have marked it.

I am John O'Rourke, and I will keep this record the way he taught record-keeping — plainly, in order, weighing nothing by what it costs the writer — and I will fail at the last of those, and let the failure stand. He said grief was owed. I pay it in the margins.

He ordered me home at four o'clock on the Monday morning. He shook my hand at his door, and thanked me for the ink, as if I had lent him a pen at the rail, and told me to sleep, and to present the instrument to Captain Deven at eight exactly, and to remember all my life that I had done nothing in those sixty hours but my duty, performed better than any man in the force could have performed it.

Then he shut the door, gently, and I walked three streets and stopped.

The record may as well know its finisher. I have never once in the pages of this book obeyed an order to stop watching, and I did not begin that morning. I stood in a doorway in the fog — his craft; everything I have is his craft — and at a quarter to five the door opened and the tall grey figure came out in its coat and hat and walked north, toward the water, unhurried, and I followed at forty yards, the worst forty yards ever kept by a watcher, because I could not close them and could not widen them and could not for my soul decide which of the two men I loved was walking ahead of me — the teacher going to his sentence, or the other one, going to its appointment.

I have asked myself every night since what I meant to do. I had no warrant, no right, no plan. I think I meant what he had taught the whole parish to mean by a coat in the dark: to be there. To stand where the paper stands.

At the head of Battery Wharf the fog was a wall, and the bell was ringing the hour, and the figure ahead of me stopped at the last of the planking, and took off its hat, and set it down square beside its boots — I found the hat after; the boots beside it, laced, evened; clerk's work, to the end — and I broke and ran the last forty yards, and I was a young man and fast, and the distance was nothing, and I did not reach him.

I will swear to what I saw in the last second of it, and then this record is done with the wharf forever. He went from the edge the way he did all things.

Not a fall. Not a flinch. A step — measured, patient, exact, a man who had checked the tide in the almanac — and the fog and the water took him with hardly a sound; quietly; the way, I came at last to understand, standing on the planking with his hat by my boots and the bell finishing the hour over the empty harbor, the way everything in his keeping had always been taken:

attended.

He knew how long it takes. God rest him. He was not hurried even then.


The harbor gave him back on the Wednesday, by the Winnisimmet ferry-ways, and the city buried him on the Saturday.

What happened between those sentences is the last case of this record, and I will rule it as he would.

Captain Deven read the instrument on the Monday at eight, as ordered, with the body not yet found and the hat from the wharf on the desk between us. He read it twice through, an hour, in a silence I have never heard the like of. And then the captain of my life's respect looked at me across those eleven sworn pages and offered, in a voice I did not know, the clean version.

The record has heard the clean version before. It was thus: the instrument to the vault; the great detective drowned on his vigil, hunting the strangler through the fog of the wharves; a hero's stone; a quiet reopening of the case in the spring; Flynn released discreetly on the island alibi, the charges lapsing for want of evidence; the parish's trust kept whole; the cathedral standing; no ruin, no rope, no reader ever. "He is dead, John," Deven said, and he was not a villain saying it; the record insists; he was a tired keeper of a frightened city, pleading for his flock in the only grammar office had left him. "The truth can do no justice now. It can only burn. I am asking what he'd ask. Think what he gave this force, and tell me what the whole truth buys that his name must pay for."

And because I was his student, I had the answer, and it was not mine, and I gave it with his own inflection, which was cruel of me, and correct.

"It buys the books, Captain. The trust was never the cathedral — that was the year's whole error, yours, mine, the parish's, his. The books are the cathedral. The complaint book a girl can walk to, the roster that answers, the record that cannot be razored — that's the whole of what stands between the frightened and the fog, and he kept those books sixty hours at his own table with the water already in his ears rather than leave one column false. The instrument is the truth in the proper form, sworn, exhibited, and closed. File it clean, sir. It's the only stone he wanted."

Deven stood a long time at his window, at his street.

"You'll go far in this work," he said at last, not turning, "and I am sorry for it," and he reached for the bell-pull, and sent for the mayor's office, and filed it clean.


Chapter Thirty-Three

The city did not burn. The record sets that down for every keeper of every cathedral who reads it after: the city did not burn.

It grieved. The instrument was published to the council on the Thursday and to the sheets on the Friday, whole, unsoftened, in his own words, with the exhibits summarized and Flynn's innocence in the first paragraph by his own instruction; and the parish that was to have been taught for months, as entertainment, that its most trusted man was the beast, learned it instead in one morning, from the man himself, in the fair strong hand, with the account already closed — and the difference was the difference he had staked his last hour on. There was no theatre to attend. There was only a grave, and the truth standing by it, and the strange stunned dignity of a wrong that arrives already judged.

They came to the funeral in thousands. I will not try the scene; he would strike the adjectives; I will keep three entries only, in the proper column.

Daniel Flynn walked from the Charles Street jail on the Thursday noon, blinking, in a coat the city bought him, to a discharge read aloud on the steps — the mayor's man had wanted it quiet and Kate Flynn had wanted it read aloud, and no power in the Commonwealth was going to beat Kate Flynn twice. She stood by her brother while it was read. Then she came through the crowd to me — I was for slipping off — and looked at me a long moment with those level Monday eyes, and said: "You're the one finished it." "I'm the one, ma'am." "Then I've a fifth sentence for you," she said. "I said it would be on the old one when it came out wrong, and it was on him, and he paid it like a man. Here's the new one, and you'll carry it, for you're young: it should never be one man's to pay. Build it so it's never one man's again. That's what you owe my brother." And she shook my hand like a magistrate and took Daniel home to Roxbury.

I have been building at her sentence ever since. This record is a course of its bricks.

Mr. Loomis I found a place for in the spring, keeper of the pressroom files at a station across the city where his history is nobody's property — the ruined are eager, and eagerness, set to honest shelves, is the best clerk alive. He knows what he guards. He asked me once, shy as ever, whether the tall still gentleman in the fog had truly been — what it turned out he'd been; and I said yes; and Loomis was quiet, and then said the thing I have thought about more than any philosophy of that whole year: "Then I was right, in a way, sir. I said I thought she had a guardian. He thought so too, you see. That's the sadness of it. We were both of us right about what he thought he was."

Captain Deven retired at the year's turn, full honors, and was not sorry to go, and told me so, and died within the two years, and the parish that spat on his young coat sixty years before sent its clergy to walk in his procession, which he would have called the trust operating as built, and would have been right.

And Nell.

Miss Mercer came back from Worcester on the Tuesday, to the house, before any word could be got to her — came back early of her own accord, because the almanac was in her blood too and her father's telegrams had gone two days strange. I met her at the depot. I had sworn to do it and would not have a stranger do it, and there, on the same platform where he'd told her the one lie, I told her the whole truth standing, every column of it, because she was his daughter and would have the account exact or count me among her enemies.

She heard it out — all of it — white and straight and dry-eyed, her two fists holding my coat front the way I'm told she held his; and when it was done she asked one question, the best question asked in the whole of this record, the question the inquiry never thought of and the sheets never printed:

"The five women. The ones whose pages just — stop. In the hidden book." Her voice was her father's, level as a rule. "Do they know? Has anyone told them what they were spared, and watched over them since, and — no. Not 'has anyone.'" The fists tightened. "Take me to them. He kept their columns. Somebody owes them the finishing."

She has visited every one of the five, quietly, these several years now. Two of them know the whole of it; three know only that a schoolteacher from Parmenter Street calls on them and their families with a friendship none of them can quite date the beginning of, and stands godmother to one's daughter — a Margaret, as it happens — and can be sent for, any hour, by any woman of those streets who is frightened of a shape in an archway.

She keeps the school. She keeps the house. She keeps the almanac.

She does not keep the third place set at table any more, she tells me, but she has been known to lay it — Sundays, in the winter mostly —

— and the record, which has now only one chapter of business left in the world, is going to allow its finisher the single unruled line he has been holding back for years, and strike it from no draft:

I have never once come up that hill of a winter Sunday and found the third place bare.


Chapter Thirty-Four

The station house on Hanover Street keeps two kinds of hours, and I have long ago chosen mine.

I have his desk now. Sullivan had it a year and could not sleep for the parish's looking, and when it came to me the sergeants watched to see would I move it from the window, and I did not, and that was the last of the watching. The force is kind to me in the manner of men being kind to a monument, which is a thing he would have hated and I have learned to use: a monument gets truth told to it at the rail that a mere detective never hears.

The books are changed. Kate Flynn's sentence, in bricks: the complaint book lives now in a locked press, its pages numbered and counted at each shift's turning, entries countersigned, the whole audited quarterly by an officer of another district who owes this house nothing — never again one man's to read alone, never again one man's to pay alone. The night rosters cross-witness. The pressroom signs its readers in a book the readers cannot reach. When the men grumble at the countersigning — they grumble; procedure is weather — I tell them a version they can carry: that we had a case once where the books were the only honest witness in the building, and we nearly lost it because only one man could hear them. It is even true. It is the record's whole truth, sized for a Tuesday.

And the walking is rostered now. That is mine, and I'll claim it plainly, this once. Any woman of this parish who files fear at our rail is offered her way home walked, by daylight-known officers in pairs, rain or fine, till her matter's closed — in pairs, always; the pairing is the point; the record's readers will know why, and the frightened need never learn. The men called it O'Rourke's courting-service for a season, and then a strange thing happened, which is that the rail got busy. The frightened came back to it. They come earlier now, at the first following, the first wrong stillness in an archway — they come, Byrne says, the way they'd come to a well that's been proved clean; and each entry is taken fairly, and countersigned, and walked home behind, and the well stays clean because no single keeper holds its key.

That is the cathedral. He knew it sixty hours before the end, and bought it with everything, and I am only its sexton. But I keep his hours.

The quiet hours, I mean. I am the one at the lamp now — one lamp, the stove ticking, the day's paper settling into the books where it will keep — and I read the complaint book the way he taught the reading, line by line, the city's fear laid still on the page; and if my own hand one day begins to keep columns I cannot account for, there is a locked press, and a counter-signature, and an auditor across the city who owes me nothing; and I have besides lodged with Nell Mercer a letter to be opened on the day I cannot answer for my nights, and she has lodged my instructions with a person she declines to name, and this is not morbidity, whatever the reader thinks. This is the method. Every man has the drawer. The books must be built for the drawer, not for the days.

The days are always whole. It is not the days that keep the books.

I finish in December, at the year's turning, at his window, late. The parish is down there under the snow-haze and the gas, going home in its thousands, trusting its streets, parting around the helmets like water and closing behind, and the record is done, and wants only its verdict, and I have sat over the verdict-line a long while tonight, because everything true that can be said of him has two faces, and he taught me never to file the one without the other.

So: both, and the reader may rule the line himself, for the reader has earned a column of his own by now.

He was the fracture. The thing we hunted all that year wore the face we trusted most, and its books were kept at our own rail, in our finest hand, and every soul in this record who slept safe a single night of that winter slept safe inside a broken man's unbroken method. That is one face, and it is true, and I do not soften it, tonight or ever.

And he was the foundation. The same hand that kept the terrible columns kept the true ones; the method that hid him caught him; there was no other detective in the Commonwealth, in the age, who could have taken the Hanover Street strangler — and one did. Alone, at his own table, at the full price, with the water in his ears and the sum come even. That is the other face, and it is also true, and I have countersigned both entries, and closed the book.

Boston has order. It returned, as it always does.

It is not the same order. It is countersigned now. It knows what drawer it was built against, which no order of this city ever knew before; and perhaps that is the only kind of order the quiet hours will honor: not the trust that stops looking, but the trust that looks, and enters fairly, and initials the margin, and lights the next man's lamp.

Nothing in this record is false.

It was the reader that failed; and the reader caught up; and the record forgives him — both of him — and is ended.

Outside, over the North End, over the harbor and the snow and the ten thousand trusting doors, the bell begins.

It does not ring sharply.

It rolls.


THE END
