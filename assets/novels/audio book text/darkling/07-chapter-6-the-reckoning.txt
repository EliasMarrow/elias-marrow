[[pause:0.72]]

There was no ceremony to it. No discussion about what it meant or what came next. There was a practical problem and they solved it practically, because that was what survival required and they had learned to solve practical problems before the feeling of them arrived.

[[pause:0.58]]

He appeared in the barn doorway ten minutes after it was over, looked at what was there — [[pause:0.18]] four men on the straw-covered floor, one of them barely more than a boy — [[pause:0.18]] and went to get tools. His face had gone the particular gray of a man doing arithmetic he never wanted to do, calculating what this meant for the town, for his store, for Ed-er, even as his hands moved to fetch shovels and canvas. He didn't speak. He didn't ask questions. He was a man who had lived long enough in a system like this to understand that there were moments when understanding could wait and the ground needed to be cleared first.

[[pause:0.34]]

Oss-uh came an hour later, his usual loud presence reduced to something quiet and efficient, his easy laugh nowhere in evidence. [[pause:0.22]] Then two other men whose names Sehr-uff didn't yet know — [[pause:0.18]] a miller's apprentice called Finn, broad-shouldered and silent, and an older man, Brask, who walked with a permanent stoop from decades bent over a plow and who worked now with the same patient stoop, digging without complaint. They worked together in silence, which was its own kind of conversation — [[pause:0.18]] the conversation of people who were past the point of discussion and into the territory of decision.

[[pause:0.34]]

The ground beyond the orchard, where the soil was soft from the irrigation channel that ran along its eastern edge, took the digging easily. Nobody spoke about where, exactly, or marked it in any way that would survive a season of rain. The work took most of the afternoon. By evening it was finished, and the barn had been swept clean, and Pell's wife had quietly burned the soiled straw in the kitchen fire along with anything else that needed burning, and the town's outward face had returned to something resembling its ordinary shape.

[[pause:0.34]]

All-drick was not among the dead. He had not been in the barn. He had been at the table in the square when the noise started — [[pause:0.18]] the particular sounds that carried even through thick walls, sounds a trained ear recognized regardless of which side of a door it stood on — [[pause:0.18]] and by the time it ended he was gone. His horse and his ledger and his careful administrative face had vanished back down the eastern road before anyone thought to stop him, the dust of his departure still hanging faintly in the air by the time Hugo and Gar emerged from the barn.

[[pause:0.34]]

He would report what happened.

[[pause:0.58]]

That was certain.

[[pause:0.72]]

The only question was how he would report it, and what language the report would use, and how long the process of response would take.

[[pause:1.80]]

All-drick rode through the night and arrived at the regional garrison two days later, hollow-eyed, his uniform stained with road dust, his careful composure intact but visibly costing him more than usual to maintain. He gave his account to a senior officer named Castellan Vair-ess — [[pause:0.18]] a heavyset man with a soldier's economy of expression, who listened without interrupting and asked few questions, because the shape of the story was familiar enough that the details barely mattered.

[[pause:0.34]]

Four men dead. The requisition incomplete. A farm administrator and at least one other resident implicated. Resistance to lawful collection.

[[pause:0.34]]

Vair-ess did not ask whether the requisition had been reasonable. That was not a question the garrison concerned itself with. He asked instead what language should go into the report, and here All-drick — [[pause:0.18]] who had, in his years of this work, learned the particular grammar that made events survivable on paper — [[pause:0.18]] supplied the proper terms without being asked twice.

[[pause:0.34]]

Disturbance.

[[pause:0.72]]

Unlawful interference with crown agents.

[[pause:0.58]]

Loss of personnel during the execution of routine duties.

[[pause:0.46]]

Nowhere in the report would it say that a boy of nineteen had drawn a blade on a man twice his size out of fear rather than orders, or that the man he'd drawn it on had wept afterward in a barn that smelled of hay and blood. Nowhere would it say that the requisition itself had asked for more than the town could give and survive the winter. The report would say what reports said, and it would move up through the proper channels at the proper pace, and somewhere weeks later a decision would be made by men who had never seen Idlewood and never would.

[[pause:0.34]]

The crown's mechanisms were not fast. That was one of the things the Gate had taught all three brothers, in different ways — [[pause:0.18]] institutions moved at the speed of paperwork, which was slower than people expected and faster than people hoped.

[[pause:0.34]]

They had time.

[[pause:0.72]]

[[pause:1.80]]

[[pause:0.58]]

This was Sehr-uff's idea. [[pause:0.22]] Not said aloud — [[pause:0.18]] just embodied, the way Sehr-uff embodied things, through simply continuing to do what needed doing. He went back to the desk the next morning. Opened the ledgers. Answered correspondence, his quill moving across the page in the same careful hand it always had, though it took him longer than usual to form the letters correctly.

[[pause:0.34]]

[[pause:0.58]]

By the third day they didn't.

[[pause:0.58]]

Hugo worked the fields. He worked harder than before, if that was possible, with the quality of a man burning something off, swinging the scythe through wheat with a force that left Oss-uh watching him sideways more than once, concerned in a way he didn't voice. Oss-uh watched him from a distance and said nothing and made sure there was always more to do — [[pause:0.18]] another field, another fence, another task that asked something physical of a man who clearly needed somewhere to put what he was carrying.

[[pause:0.34]]

Gar moved through the town as he always had. Present. Purposeful. Saying nothing that didn't need saying, which was everything. If anything, he seemed steadier than before, his attention more deliberate, as though the barn had simply confirmed something he had always quietly understood about the shape of the world.

[[pause:0.34]]

The town held the secret the way small towns hold secrets that implicate everyone — [[pause:0.18]] not comfortably, not easily, but with the understanding that the alternative was worse. People who had been in the square that day had seen what happened before the barn. They had seen Sehr-uff try to negotiate, watched him offer alternative after alternative with the ledgers open and his voice steady even as his face bled. They had seen what the guards did when negotiation was finished.

[[pause:0.34]]

They carried that.

[[pause:0.72]]

It sat in them differently than other things sat in them. [[pause:0.22]] Not the weight of complicity exactly. Something more complicated — [[pause:0.18]] the weight of having witnessed something true that nobody was going to be allowed to call true, at least not out loud, at least not yet.

[[pause:0.34]]

Cal-uh, who had stood frozen at the well with her laundry basket through the whole of the beating, found she could not look at her own hands for several days afterward without remembering how useless they had felt, how desperately she had wanted to move and how completely she had understood that moving would have cost more than it saved. She said nothing of this to anyone. She simply began bringing more food than before to the storage room above the mill, as though the extra portions were a kind of penance for a debt she couldn't otherwise name.

[[pause:0.34]]

Toe-bren, the old chapel-keeper, lit a candle that he did not explain to anyone and let it burn down on its own through that first night.

[[pause:0.34]]

Pell did not sleep well for a week. His wife found him twice standing in the dark kitchen, simply standing, his hands flat on the counter, and both times she said nothing and simply stood beside him until he was ready to come back to bed.

[[pause:0.34]]

Ed-er found Sehr-uff at the desk one evening near the end of the second week.

[[pause:0.46]]

[[pause:0.58]]

[[pause:0.58]]

Ed-er was quiet for a moment. "Are you going to leave?"

[[pause:0.58]]

Sehr-uff looked at him. At the serious face, the careful eyes — [[pause:0.18]] the face of a child who had grown up in a place where the wrong question had consequences and had learned to ask them anyway, because some questions mattered more than the consequences of asking.

[[pause:0.34]]

"I don't know," Sehr-uff said honestly.

[[pause:0.58]]

[[pause:0.34]]

The second group of men arrived on a morning three weeks later, the air sharper now with the first true edge of autumn, frost silvering the grass at dawn before the sun burned it away.

[[pause:0.34]]

More of them this time. Six riders, not four, and their bearing was different — [[pause:0.18]] less administrative, more deliberate, the loose easy posture of routine collection replaced by something tighter, more purposeful. These were not men coming to collect a number. These were men coming because something had happened that required a different kind of response, and they rode into the square with the particular grim efficiency of soldiers given an actual task rather than a tedious one.

[[pause:0.34]]

Their leader was a man named Kor-en — [[pause:0.18]] older than All-drick, harder around the eyes, a career soldier rather than an administrator, the kind of man the garrison sent when a situation called for results rather than paperwork. He had been told, in broad terms, what had happened in this town three weeks prior. He had not been told the full truth, because nobody at the garrison knew the full truth, only the shape of it that All-drick's report had been willing to commit to paper. He believed, more or less, that the town harbored men who had murdered crown agents. He believed this the way a soldier believes the orders he is given — [[pause:0.18]] not blindly, but without the energy required to question them, because questioning orders was not a skill the garrison particularly rewarded.

[[pause:0.34]]

"The first blow was meant to be the beginning of a process. The kind of pressure that had worked in enough other places that it had become routine. You found the weakest point and you applied force until the structure gave way. Everyone had a weakest point. That was the theory. That was what the job had taught.

[[pause:0.34]]

Gar's weakest point, if he had one, was somewhere they couldn't reach.

[[pause:0.46]]

They beat him in the yard outside the mill.

[[pause:0.58]]

The town watched from windows and doorways — [[pause:0.18]] Pell, his hand pressed white-knuckled to the frame; [[pause:0.16]] Cal-uh, her basket dropped and forgotten at her feet a second time; [[pause:0.16]] Oss-uh, his jaw working, his whole body taut with the same restraint that had held the square silent weeks before; [[pause:0.16]] Ed-er, who had not gone to his fence post that morning and instead stood frozen in his own doorway, his mother's hand gripping his shoulder hard enough to leave marks, both of them watching anyway because some things could not be looked away from no matter how much they should be. They watched in the silence of people who understood the calculation and could not make it come out differently and hated themselves for that understanding.

[[pause:0.34]]

At the far corner of the yard, half hidden behind the woodpile, stood a child nobody could place — [[pause:0.18]] small, hooded against the morning cold, still in a way that children are not usually still. Beneath the edge of the wool, if anyone had been looking, the suggestion of horns. Nobody was looking. When the guards finally rode off, the corner was empty, and no one could have said when it had emptied.

He did not speak.

[[pause:0.72]]

This was the thing that confused them — [[pause:0.18]] you could see it in the guards' faces, the particular confusion of men whose tool was not working. Pain was supposed to produce fear and fear was supposed to produce cooperation and the sequence was not completing itself. The man on the ground was not afraid. Or if he was, the fear was somewhere so deep inside him that it had no surface, no access point, no way out — [[pause:0.18]] a fear that had been folded so far down, so many years ago, beneath a vow he had made for reasons none of them would ever know, that it no longer had any language left to speak in, even under this.

[[pause:0.34]]

Kor-en tried longer than the others would have. He had broken men before — [[pause:0.18]] not many, but enough to believe in the method, to trust that pain applied correctly and patiently always eventually found the seam. He waited for the seam. He kept waiting.

[[pause:0.72]]

[[pause:0.58]]

Not dead. [[pause:0.22]] But finished, for now, in the way a body was finished when it had given everything available to give, his breathing shallow and ragged in the cold morning air, blood darkening into the dirt beneath him.

Kor-en looked down at Gar for a long moment. Something crossed his face that might have been respect, might have been disgust at himself, might have simply been the particular blankness of a man who had run out of certainty about what he was supposed to feel.
