Chapter Five. Idlewood.
[[pause:1.20]]

The town did not know what to make of them at first.

[[pause:0.58]]

That was fine.

[[pause:0.72]]

They did not know what to make of the town either.

[[pause:0.58]]

Idlewood sat in a shallow valley between two long ridges, the kind of place geography had made useful before anyone decided to settle it — [[pause:0.18]] low enough to catch runoff from both slopes, sheltered enough from the worst of the northern wind, close enough to the eastern road that goods could move without the town ever quite becoming a crossroads itself. Fields stretched outward from the central cluster of buildings in every direction — [[pause:0.18]] wheat mostly, gold-going-brown in the late summer light, some root vegetables in long mounded rows, orchards on the eastern slope that produced more than the town consumed and exactly as much as the crown's requisition orders expected, no more, no less, the arithmetic of survival worked out over generations until it had become instinct rather than calculation. The buildings were old but maintained, timber gone silver-gray with weather, roofs patched in places with newer wood that hadn't yet weathered to match. The roads were dirt but kept, raked free of the worst ruts after each rain, because a wagon stuck to its axle was a half-day lost that the town's margins couldn't absorb. Everything about the place had the quality of something that had learned to survive by being exactly what was required of it and nothing more — [[pause:0.18]] not poor enough to be abandoned, not prosperous enough to attract real attention, useful in the particular unremarkable way that kept a place safe by making it forgettable.

[[pause:0.34]]

They arrived at the edge of it on a morning in early autumn, three men who looked like what they were — [[pause:0.18]] escaped from something, traveled hard, carrying nothing. Sehr-uff walked on his own by then, still pale, still quieter than usual, the venom's last traces working their way out of him slowly, but upright. Hugo moved like a man expecting the ground to shift beneath him, his eyes cataloguing exits and angles out of habit he didn't examine. Gar simply moved, the way he always moved, present without announcing it.

[[pause:0.34]]

The fields they crossed smelled of cut hay and warm dust, a smell so far removed from the Gate's cold stone and the Darkwood's wet rot that for a moment Sehr-uff found himself simply standing still in it, breathing, unable to immediately place what the feeling rising in his chest was. It took him a moment to recognize it as something close to grief — [[pause:0.18]] not for anything lost, but for how long it had been since the world had offered him a smell that didn't carry threat folded into it.

[[pause:0.34]]

"My father runs the grain store," he said finally. "He knows everyone."

[[pause:0.46]]

[[pause:0.40]]

[[pause:0.34]]

[[pause:0.40]]

[[pause:0.58]]

[[pause:1.80]]

They stayed.

[[pause:0.72]]

It happened gradually, the way things that last tend to happen — [[pause:0.18]] not with ceremony or decision but with the accumulation of ordinary days until leaving would have required more intention than staying.

[[pause:0.34]]

Sehr-uff inherited the dead administrator's desk and his chaos. The man's name, Sehr-uff learned eventually from a stray letter, had been Kor-vin, and he had apparently been well liked and poorly organized in roughly equal measure — [[pause:0.18]] the filing system was not a system. It was a record of someone's good intentions repeatedly interrupted by crisis. Forms were misfiled or missing entirely, stuffed into drawers that didn't match their labels, as though Kor-vin had developed, over years, an ever-more-elaborate private logic that made sense only to him and had died with him. Correspondence from the crown's requisition office went back three years with gaps that suggested the previous administrator had hoped certain letters would resolve themselves if ignored long enough.

[[pause:0.34]]

They hadn't.

[[pause:0.72]]

Sehr-uff worked.

[[pause:0.72]]

He worked the way he did everything — [[pause:0.18]] methodically, without complaint, with a quality of attention that made the people who watched him feel that the thing he was attending to mattered. He reorganized the ledgers, his ink-stained fingers moving across page after page in the small cramped office that smelled of dust and old paper and, faintly, of the lamp oil he burned late into the evenings. He answered the correspondence. He submitted the corrected forms and flagged the discrepancies and wrote careful, precise letters to the requisition office that were not defiant but were extremely clear about what Idlewood could and could not provide, each sentence weighed before it was set to paper, each number checked twice against the ledger before it left his hands.

[[pause:0.34]]

The fines stopped.

[[pause:0.72]]

This was not a small thing.

[[pause:0.34]]

[[pause:0.34]]

[[pause:0.58]]

[[pause:0.58]]

[[pause:0.34]]

[[pause:1.80]]

Hugo found work the same day he looked for it.

[[pause:0.58]]

The harvest was coming. Idlewood needed bodies and Hugo was a body in the way that certain bodies simply solved problems by existing. He worked from before dawn to after dark without complaint and with a consistency that farm labor valued above almost any other quality. He didn't require instruction after the first day. He didn't need managing. He simply appeared at the edge of a field and worked across it — [[pause:0.18]] his hands finding the rhythm of the scythe within an hour, the particular economy of motion that turned exhausting work into something almost meditative once the body learned it — [[pause:0.18]] and when the field was done he looked for the next one.

[[pause:0.34]]

The farmhands were cautious at first.

[[pause:0.58]]

A half-dwarf with a braid threaded with gray and hands like carved wood, who said almost nothing and worked like he was settling a debt.

[[pause:0.34]]

By the third day they stopped being cautious.

[[pause:0.58]]

By the end of the first week a man named Oss-uh, who had worked Pell's fields for twenty years and was generally considered to have an opinion on everything whether or not anyone had asked for it, told anyone who would listen that the quiet one was the best harvest hand he'd seen in a decade and he didn't care what he was. Oss-uh had a voice that carried across two fields without effort and a laugh that came easily and often, and within a month he had appointed himself, without consultation, as something like Hugo's unofficial translator to the rest of the town — [[pause:0.18]] explaining, to anyone who asked, that no, the big one wasn't simple, he just didn't waste words, which Oss-uh seemed to regard as a moral virtue he himself had never managed to achieve.

[[pause:0.34]]

Hugo ate with the farmhands at the long table outside the grain store, the evening meal a loose communal thing of bread and whatever stew Pell's wife had going, the table crowded with elbows and laughter and the particular companionable noise of tired bodies winding down together. He listened more than he spoke. He laughed occasionally — [[pause:0.18]] not often, but genuinely, when something was actually funny rather than as performance. He drank moderately, nursing the same cup of Oss-uh's homebrew through an entire evening while men around him refilled theirs three and four times. He slept well.

[[pause:0.34]]

He hadn't slept well in years.

[[pause:0.58]]

He didn't think about that directly. He just noticed, in the morning, that the first thing he felt was not threat assessment. That was new. He would lie there for a moment, in the gray light through the storage room's single window, simply registering the absence of the old reflexive alertness, before getting up to start the day.

[[pause:1.80]]

Gar moved through the town like weather.

[[pause:0.58]]

Present. Purposeful. [[pause:0.22]] Not quite belonging to any particular place but not out of place either. He helped where help was needed without being asked and without requiring acknowledgment. He fixed a fence on the north field that had been leaning for a season, resetting the posts with a patience that made the work look easier than it was. He helped a woman named Cal-uh move grain sacks when her regular help didn't come, lifting weights that should have required two men with the same economical effort he'd once spent on dead rats in a Gate corridor. He sat with Ed-er sometimes in the evenings outside the mill while the boy talked about things boys talked about — [[pause:0.18]] animals, weather, the unfairness of specific adults, a complicated ongoing rivalry with another boy over a matter of marbles that Ed-er narrated with the gravity of a war — [[pause:0.18]] and Gar listened and occasionally nodded and Ed-er seemed to find this perfectly satisfying, perhaps the most patient audience he'd ever had.

[[pause:0.34]]

The town learned his silence the way you learn a neighbor's habits. [[pause:0.22]] Not explained. Just known.

[[pause:0.46]]

A few people tried to draw him out in the early weeks. Oss-uh asked him a direct question once, a test of sorts — [[pause:0.18]] something about whether he preferred the scythe or the sickle, an opinion Oss-uh clearly held strongly himself — [[pause:0.18]] and received only a look in return that was not hostile but was complete. [[pause:0.34]]

Cal-uh brought them food sometimes. Left it without announcement — [[pause:0.18]] a covered dish on the storage room's outer step, gone before anyone thanked her for it. The gesture of a woman who had lived long enough to recognize people who had come from hard places without needing to hear the story, who had perhaps come from one herself, though nobody in town spoke of it directly, and who seemed to understand instinctively that the kindest thing she could offer wasn't conversation but its absence.

[[pause:0.34]]

There was an old man, Toe-bren, who tended the town's small chapel — [[pause:0.18]] less a building than a converted barn with a modest altar at one end, used more for funerals and the rare wedding than regular worship, Idlewood not being a particularly devout place in any organized sense. Toe-bren watched Gar once, working in the chapel yard without being asked, clearing fallen branches after a storm, and said nothing to him directly, but mentioned afterward to Pell that he'd never seen a man work like that — [[pause:0.18]] like the work itself was a kind of prayer, though Toe-bren couldn't have said toward what.

The crown's men arrived on a Tuesday.

[[pause:0.58]]

There was no announcement, no advance warning — [[pause:0.18]] just the sound of horses on the road from the east, hooves on packed dirt carrying further than they should have in the still morning air, and then four men in the crown's colors riding into the center of Idlewood like they owned it, which in most practical senses they did. [[pause:0.34]]

Their leader was a man named All-drick. He was not old and not young, not cruel-looking and not kind.
