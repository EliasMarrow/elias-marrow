Participation

On how engagement becomes legitimacy

By Elias Marrow

← All essaysRead on Substack ↗

I noticed the absence before I noticed the event.

Not because nothing had happened, but because something had—and it was not appearing where it usually would. The feed did not react the way it had learned to react. There was no repetition. No flood. No unavoidable confrontation. I had to scroll. Then scroll again. Then search.

When stories matter in the way we are told they matter, they do not hide. They surface immediately, everywhere, dressed in clarity. This one did not.

The name appeared eventually. It had to. Omission would have been conspicuous. But the name functioned more like a credential than a center. A brief acknowledgment. Age. Occupation. Then a turn away.

The gravity of attention moved quickly—to agencies, to officials, to laws, to institutions. To abstractions large enough to carry argument without requiring empathy. The person receded. The system took their place.

This was not unique to one side.

Progressive coverage did it. Conservative coverage did it. Different tones, same structure. Different conclusions, identical movement. The victim was named, then bypassed. Identity was present, then neutralized. The story survived only after it stopped being about the person.

This is where people reach for ideology as explanation. But ideology is not doing the work here. Incentives are.

Ambiguous victims do not travel well. They slow people down. They require hesitation. And hesitation is visible—measurable—fatal to amplification. When moral clarity collapses, engagement certainty collapses with it. The feed does not punish ambiguity out of malice. It simply starves it.

So narratives adapt.

They become about institutions, because institutions are scalable. They become about authority, because authority is legible. They become about law, because law offers conflict without intimacy. The human cost is acknowledged just enough to maintain legitimacy, then set aside so the story can move.

This happens whether the conclusion is condemnation or defense. Whether the tone is outrage or concern. Whether the speaker wears the language of justice or order. The surface differs. The mechanics do not.

This is why choosing sides feels meaningful but changes nothing.

Side-taking produces identity, not leverage. It supplies energy, not friction. It keeps attention cycling inside a frame that was never designed to absorb challenge to itself. The system does not need consensus. It needs participation.

Arguing sustains it. Condemning sustains it. Defending sustains it. Even resisting sustains it—so long as resistance accepts the arena, the language, the cadence of response.

The most stabilizing act is not agreement. It is continued engagement.

This is the quiet truth beneath the noise: legitimacy does not come from justice. It comes from repetition. From presence. From participation that never exits the frame.

The system does not persist because it is right.
It persists because people keep participating in ways that make it appear necessary.

— Elias Marrow
