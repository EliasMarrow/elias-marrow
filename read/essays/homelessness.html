Homelessness

A societal truth.

By Elias Marrow

← All essaysRead on Substack ↗

Not a Moral Failure

There is a reflex most people have when they see someone sleeping under an overpass.

A quiet calculation.

What did they do wrong?

Drugs.
Bad choices.
No discipline.
Didn’t work hard enough.
Burned bridges.
Refused help.

It feels cleaner that way.
If homelessness is a character flaw, then it is containable.
If it is personal failure, then it is avoidable.
If it is avoidable, then we are safe.

But what if it is none of those things?

What if homelessness is not a deviation from the system — but an output of it?

The Comfort of Blame

Blame is stabilizing.

If a man on the sidewalk is there because he made reckless decisions, then the system remains intact. The rules are fair. The ladder exists. The bottom is simply where people fall when they refuse to climb.

But that story collapses under scale.

In the United States, over half a million people experience homelessness on any given night. Millions more hover one missed paycheck away. In many cities, full-time workers sleep in cars. Families rotate motel rooms. Veterans live in tents. Children attend school without permanent addresses.

It strains logic to believe this is a synchronized outbreak of personal irresponsibility.

When a problem becomes statistically widespread, it stops being anecdotal. It becomes structural.

Systems Produce What They Incentivize

Every system produces predictable outputs based on its design.

Our housing market rewards speculation and asset appreciation.
Our labor market rewards flexibility over stability.
Our healthcare system ties access to employment.
Our safety nets are thin, conditional, and bureaucratically complex.
Our zoning laws restrict supply.
Our wages lag behind rent in major metros.

None of these are accidents.
They are incentives aligned toward efficiency, capital growth, and mobility.

Homelessness emerges where those incentives intersect with volatility.

Lose a job in a high-rent market.
Miss two weeks due to illness.
Face a medical bill without insurance.
Experience domestic violence.
Age out of foster care.
Return from prison with limited access to employment.
Suffer untreated mental illness.
Fall into addiction in a system where treatment is scarce and housing requires sobriety first.

The system does not need to be malicious to produce collapse.

It only needs to optimize for something other than human stability.

Scarcity Changes Decision-Making

There is another layer people ignore.

Poverty and housing instability alter cognition.

When survival becomes immediate, time horizons shrink. Certainty becomes more valuable than optimization. Risk tolerance shifts. Decisions that look irrational from stability can be survival-optimized under scarcity.

A person with savings can compare job offers.
A person with no savings takes the first available income.
A person with housing can wait for treatment.
A person on the street cannot pause for long-term recovery.

When every choice is made under pressure, outcomes compound.

Calling that “bad decision-making” misunderstands the environment in which decisions are made.

The Myth of the Outlier

We like to imagine the homeless population as fundamentally different from the housed population.

But data consistently shows something uncomfortable:

A large percentage of Americans could not cover a $1,000 emergency without debt or borrowing.
Rent burdens exceed 30–50% of income for millions.
Medical debt is one of the leading causes of bankruptcy.
Wages have not kept pace with housing costs in many regions for decades.

The distance between housed and unhoused is often measured in months, not morality.

The difference is margin.

Margin is the buffer between disruption and collapse.

When margin disappears, volatility becomes destiny.

“But What About Addiction?”

Yes — addiction is present.

So is mental illness.

So is trauma.

But here is the part rarely acknowledged:

Stable housing dramatically improves recovery rates.
Housing-first models consistently outperform punishment-first models.
Treatment adherence increases when basic safety is secured.
People stabilize when the floor stops moving.

If addiction were purely a moral failing, housing would not improve it.

Yet it does.

Which suggests the environment matters.

A Broken System Doesn’t Mean Evil Architects

Acknowledging structural failure does not require conspiracy.

There is no hidden council orchestrating suffering.

There are incentives.

Housing as investment.
Healthcare as market commodity.
Labor as flexible input.
Growth measured by GDP rather than resilience.
Policy shaped by voters who fear property devaluation more than encampments.

Systems drift toward what they reward.

And ours rewards extraction and efficiency more than redundancy and stability.

Homelessness is what happens when efficiency outpaces margin.

The Psychological Function of Distance

Blaming individuals serves another purpose.

It protects the belief that effort guarantees security.

If homelessness is proof of laziness, then working hard immunizes you.

But if homelessness is proof of structural volatility, then effort alone is not sufficient protection.

That realization is destabilizing.

So we simplify.

We say:
“They chose that.”
“They don’t want help.”
“They’re all addicts.”
“They prefer the street.”

It is easier than admitting that a fragile system produces casualties without villainy.

What Would a Functional System Look Like?

A system designed around stability would include:

Affordable housing supply aligned with wage reality.
Healthcare untethered from employment volatility.
Treatment integrated with housing.
Wages indexed to cost of living.
Simplified safety nets that reduce administrative friction.
Zoning reform that increases density without political paralysis.
Redundancy over pure efficiency.

These are not utopian fantasies. They are design choices.

Other countries have made different ones.

The question is not whether homelessness is inevitable.

The question is what a system prioritizes.

The Moral Reframe

Seeing homelessness as personal failure isolates the individual.

Seeing it as systemic output changes the frame entirely.

It shifts the conversation from:
“What is wrong with them?”

to:
“What does this say about us?”

Homelessness is not evidence of bad people.

It is evidence of narrow margins.

It is proof that when stability is commodified, those who cannot afford it are exposed.

It is a structural signal.

And signals are not meant to be shamed.

They are meant to be studied.

If we continue to treat homelessness as a moral defect, we will continue to misdiagnose the disease.

And systems, left unexamined, keep producing exactly what they are designed to produce.

The tents under the highway are not an anomaly.

They are data.
