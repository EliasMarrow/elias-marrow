Constraints Binding

Auto Biography

By Elias Marrow

← All essaysRead on Substack ↗

I was born in 1989, into a family that was already fragmented enough to make belonging conditional. I was the middle child, though even that designation never felt stable. There was an older sister. Then me. Then a brother who would die. Then two younger sisters. All of us shared a mother. Only my brother and I shared a father. The rest were assembled through circumstance rather than continuity.

From early on, life did not present itself as something you stepped into. It pressed inward.

Before I had language for it, instability was already normal. My mother was struggling in Roanoke, Virginia—financially, emotionally, chemically. She chose a path she believed would lead away from drugs and toward stability. That path led to Phoenix, Arizona, and to a man who, by all meaningful measures, was a good man. The house was calm. Predictable. Safe. For a brief period, life resembled what other people seemed to take for granted.

Then my brother drowned in the family swimming pool.

I was there that day.

I do not remember it as a dramatic rupture. There was no cinematic moment where the world split in half. What followed was quieter and worse. Something in my mother collapsed. Not immediately, not visibly—but decisively. We moved back to Roanoke. Over time, she disappeared from my life. Not through a single act of abandonment, but through erosion. I was too young to understand what was happening, only old enough to know that it was permanent.

I was taken in by my grandmother.

She lived alone in a one-bedroom house and walked to work. She had grown up during the Great Depression. She had raised four children by herself. Then she raised me. There was no excess in that home—only routine, restraint, and endurance. We lived on low income. Food stamps. No safety nets. No sense that help arrived when you needed it. My grandmother survived on religion and hope. Not metaphorically. Literally. Those were the only resources that never ran out.

Poverty was not just a lack of money. It was an environment that shaped behavior long before it shaped outcomes. It shortened time horizons, making long-term planning feel abstract and immediate survival feel rational. It reframed risk so that legality and safety were no longer aligned. It trained endurance, invisibility, and self-reliance rather than ambition or trust. Under poverty, effort is constant but opportunity is not, and the difference between stability and collapse is often determined by access rather than character.

By the time I was five, I had already learned something most people never consciously articulate: that I was poor, that I was parentless, and that this placed me outside whatever invisible circle other children occupied. I did not fit. I would not fit. There was no transition period where I believed otherwise.

When I was five, something happened under adult supervision that permanently altered my understanding of safety. It was not violent in the way people imagine violence, but it taught me early that silence could be protective, that authority did not guarantee care, and that endurance was often the only reliable response.

From that point forward, I learned to close myself off. Not in a dramatic way. Quietly. Functionally. I learned how to ignore. How to endure. How to minimize need. These were not personality traits. They were techniques.

As I got older, the world did not widen. It narrowed.

Money was always the problem behind the problem. When I eventually started selling marijuana, it was not rebellion. It was arithmetic. It was easy. It was available. It worked. Under the constraints I lived within, it was one of the few levers that produced predictable results. I was not chasing status or danger. I was solving a problem the only way that seemed to actually solve it.

That choice followed me.

Felony convictions did not arrive as a surprise. They arrived as confirmation. Doors that were already heavy became sealed. Legitimate paths to advancement disappeared. The language of “potential” stopped applying to me. I became someone the system could categorize quickly and discard efficiently.

What replaced ambition was a persistent sense of entrapment.

Was it my fault? Were these outcomes really the result of choice? Or were they the consequence of constrained option sets stacking over time—poverty, loss, disappearance, exclusion—until only a narrow band of survivable behavior remained?

That question would not let go of me.

It followed me into adulthood, into work, into every failed attempt to “start over” inside structures that did not forget. It became the lens through which I began to see everything else: economics, politics, punishment, rhetoric. I started to notice how often effort was praised where opportunity was absent, how responsibility was demanded where power was withheld.

Eventually, I stopped trying to prove that I deserved a different outcome.

Instead, I tried to understand the system that produced this one.

That shift—away from self-blame and toward structural analysis—is what led me here. To writing. To simulations. To long-form thought. To refusing the algorithmic incentives that reward outrage and simplicity over accuracy and depth.

My work is not about absolution. It is not about redemption. It is about accounting for reality without pretending it is kinder than it is.

I am not interested in stories where everything happens for a reason. I am interested in stories where things happen because constraints exist—and where people behave exactly as you would expect once those constraints are made visible.

This autobiography is not a justification. It is a record.

And it is the foundation from which everything else I write proceeds.
