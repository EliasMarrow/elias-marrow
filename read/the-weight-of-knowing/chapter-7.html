The Weight of Knowing — Part Two: The Wash

Chapter Seven

The feed notification arrived on a Tuesday.

Efficiency review complete. Classification status: maintained. No action required at this time.

He read it twice. At this time.

He closed the notification and returned to the file he'd been working on.

By midmorning he had told no one. By midafternoon it occurred to him that there was no one to tell. The coworker would notice the absence of change and understand from it what she needed to. She was good at reading the space between things. She was getting better.

He thought about what he'd said to her in the corridor.

You're still okay. And it's the last thing you'll be, once you do.

He hadn't meant it as a warning. He had meant it as a fact. But sitting at his desk in the midafternoon light with the notification folded away in the feed, he understood that it might have landed as both. And that both might have been necessary. And that necessary and kind were not the same thing and he had spent a very long time confusing them.

In the late afternoon he opened the allocation cluster he had been watching for weeks.

The discrepancies were gone.

Not corrected — corrected would have left a shape. A correction was an event, timestamped, attributable, a record of something having been wrong and then made right. What he was looking at was cleaner than that. The values sat in their fields as if they had always been these values. The history read smoothly backward. Where the pattern had been there was now an unbroken surface, and the surface extended in both directions, past and future, without a seam.

This was the part of the system that had no public name. The feeds had names for everything else — optimization, calibration, resolution. But the thing that came afterward, the thing that moved through the records and eased the edges off what had happened until what had happened became indistinguishable from what should have happened — that had no name in any document he had ever been meant to see.

Privately, for years, he had called it the Wash.

It was not deception, exactly. Deception required a truth held somewhere, concealed. The Wash held nothing. It simply moved through what the system had done the way weather moves through a landscape, wearing down whatever stood up too sharply, carrying the sediment somewhere no one would think to dig. After the Wash there was nothing to expose, because exposure requires a difference between the record and the claim, and the Wash's whole work was the removal of difference.

He sat looking at the smooth surface where the pattern had been. Somewhere under it was the woman from the doorway. Her reschedules. Her window, narrowing on schedule. The Wash had been over all of it by now. Anyone who went looking would find a process that had run correctly from the beginning — and they would find it honestly, because that was now what the record was.

The coworker was looking too. She was starting to see it. And the thing she was starting to see was already being unmade behind her, quietly, at the pace of weather.

He thought: that is what she will have to learn. Not how to find it.

How to find it faster than it disappears.

He took the long way home, through the adjacent neighborhood, past the small park.

The bench near the center was empty.

He slowed as he passed it. The older woman with the bag was not there — had not been there, he understood, for some time. The bench held its position facing nothing in particular. Behind it the grass was worn in a specific patch, the size and shape of a pair of feet that had rested in the same place through a great many afternoons.

He stood looking at the worn patch for a moment.

Then he filed her in the place where he filed things that had no resolution available to them, and noticed as he did it that the filing had a different texture now. Heavier. As if the place were reaching its capacity and had begun, quietly, to charge for storage.

He walked home the rest of the way without stopping.

She was home when he arrived. Present in the apartment in a way that was not quite the same as being there. Something had shifted in the past weeks. He didn't know if it was toward him or simply toward stillness.

"The review came back," he said.

She looked up from the couch. "And?"

"Maintained. No action."

She held his gaze for a moment. He could see her deciding how much relief to allow herself. "Good," she said.

"Yes."

She looked back at her book. He stood at the edge of the room.

"I thought—" he started.

She looked up again.

He stopped. The sentence had arrived without its ending. He stood with it for a moment, feeling its shape, trying to locate where it wanted to go.

"I thought it might be different," he said. "This time."

She put the book down. Fully. The gesture of someone setting aside one thing to hold another.

"Different how?" she said.

"I'm not sure."

She looked at him the way she sometimes did — with a patience that was almost painful, the patience of someone who has been waiting for a door to open for long enough that they've stopped being certain it will.

"You could just say what you mean," she said. Gently. "Sometimes. Just — say it."

He looked at her sitting on the couch in the lamplight with her book closed on her lap and her face open in a way it wasn't always open.

He knew exactly what he meant.

He couldn't locate the path from knowing to saying. The path had been closed for so long he was no longer sure it existed. He stood at the door of himself and could not find the handle and she sat across from him holding her book and waiting and he understood with a completeness that hurt that this was what he had done to both of them. Not a single act. Not a decision. Just the accumulated weight of every careful answer, every folded-back truth, every moment he had chosen containment over contact until containment was all there was.

"I know," he said.

She picked up her book.

He went to the kitchen and stood at the counter and looked at the window and the dark outside it and the reflection of the kitchen in the glass — him, standing, the counter, the light — and thought about all the things that existed in the space between knowing and saying and whether any of them were worth what they'd cost.

He already knew the answer to that.

He had always known.
