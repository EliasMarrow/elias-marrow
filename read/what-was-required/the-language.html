Chapter Two — The Language

The first thing the beam taught a hewer was how to talk to it.

Not to Marek. To the beam. The men had long ago worked out that the clerk was only the beam's mouth, and so they addressed the machinery through him, in a grammar the pit had been refining for a generation before Marek ever sat in the cabin.

"Beam's reading light today," a man would say. Not: you have robbed me. Not even: that tub was full. The beam was reading light — as if the beam were weather, as if it had moods that no man set and no man answered for. Marek would look at the needle, and look at the tub, and if there was anything to be done he would do it, and if there was nothing to be done he would say, "She reads what she reads," and the hewer would nod and take his docket and go, and both of them would have said everything while saying nothing, and no word would exist afterward that a foreman could pick up and carry away.

That was the point. That was the whole engineering of it.

Because words, once spoken in the yard, stopped belonging to the man who said them. Marek had watched it happen. A hewer named Aldis, two winters back, had stood in front of the screens and said in plain anger that the Company's scale was a thief — said it hot, said it once — and the words had gone into the underlooker's report, and from the report into the agent's letter-book, and eight months later, when the east headings were slack and men were being turned off, Aldis's name was on the list, and the letter-book was why, though no letter ever said so. The words had gone in as temper and come back wearing a uniform. Every man in the yard understood the lesson without ever discussing it, which was itself the lesson: the yard heard everything, wrote some things, and forgot nothing it wrote.

So the men spoke to the beam, and the beam could not testify.

On carpet, it was different. Marek had stood on the agent's carpet exactly four times in eleven years, and he had noticed, each time, what the carpet did to speech — his own included.

Mr. Fenn, the Company's viewer, spoke in first drafts. That was the thing Marek had carried out of that office and turned over for years. Fenn said no whole, in one syllable, and let it sit there unsoftened. He said you will and I want and that doesn't interest me. His sentences went out the door the same shape they were born, because nothing in the world stood ready to make him pay for them. Everyone else in the valley spoke in third drafts — Marek could hear the revisions inside the sentences, the way you could see old workings inside a hill. You need to mend this scale became, by the time it reached Fenn's carpet, I wondered, sir, whether the beam might be looked at, when convenient. The demand dissolved into a question. The actor vanished from the verb. An exit was cut at every joint of the sentence, so that the speaker could withdraw from any part of it the moment weather threatened.

A man who had never needed that grammar would hear it as servility, or as cunning. It was neither. It was armor, fitted to a body that had been hit before, and like all armor it recorded precisely the blows that had shaped it. Marek could read a man's history in his hedging the way Josef could read a seam in a handful of coal. The deeper the softening, the older the bruise.

If you wanted to find power in any room in the valley, you did not watch who talked most. You watched who edited least.

Sunday afternoons, Marek wrote letters.

It had begun years ago as a favor and hardened into an institution: the clerk's table by the window, the queue of neighbors with their documents in cloth wrappings, and Marek with pen and ink doing the row's translating. The Company wrote to its people constantly — notices, demands, adjustments of rent, summonses to the office — and the people wrote back through Marek, because half the row had no letters at all and the other half had letters but not these letters, not the dialect the offices required.

Because each office had its own tongue, and the offices granted nothing to requests made in the wrong one. The relief club answered only to its forms of words — a widow wrote respectfully petitions the consideration of the committee or the committee did not consider. The magistrate's clerk wanted humbly sheweth. The Company's office wanted brevity, dates, and no adjectives, and above all wanted no sentence that assigned an action to a person — nothing that said your underlooker shorted the timber, everything in the form it appears the timber allocation was found insufficient. Marek kept the phrases the way another man kept tools, oiled and ordered, and he fitted them to his neighbors' troubles one at a time.

This Sunday it was Dess at the table, with her book from the store and her debt-paper from the office, wanting a letter asking the Company to set her boy Col's putting wage against her late husband's account.

"Say it however it needs saying," she told him. "You know the way of it."

He knew the way of it. He took a fresh sheet and began, and Dess watched the pen the way people watched conjurers.

What she wanted to say was: You buried my man and then you charged me rent on the month he died in, and my son hauls your coal at fifteen for a boy's wage, and the arithmetic you have wrapped me in cannot be paid out of a screen-woman's fourpence, and you know it cannot, and you built it so it cannot.

What Marek wrote was: Respectfully sheweth, that the account standing against the late Havel Dessin being beyond the family's present means, it is humbly asked whether the wages of Col Dessin, putter, might be received against the said account, in such proportion as the Company may think fit.

Every phrase had a purpose. Respectfully sheweth unlocked the reading at all. Beyond present means — never unjust, never impossible — said broke without saying wronged, because wronged put the office on trial and the office acquitted itself by not replying. As the Company may think fit handed the whole decision over with a bow, which was the only posture from which the office ever granted anything, because a grant made to a bowing head cost the Company nothing but coin, while a grant made to an argument conceded that there had been something to argue about.

An honest account was full of handles — names, blames, the true hard words — and every handle was a thing the office could grip and use against the teller. A smooth account gave no grip. Between honesty and safety, Marek chose safety on his neighbors' behalf every Sunday of his life, and then heard the office's men say, over their pipes, that colliery folk were a sly, close-mouthed breed from whom you never got a straight story. The Company required transparency of the row and practiced discretion in the office, and saw no contradiction, having a different word for each.

Dess signed her mark at the bottom. She could not read what she was signing. She trusted him the way the whole row trusted him, which was a weight he put on with his coat every morning.

"Will it answer?" she asked.

"It may. It's in the right words now."

"Queer, isn't it," Dess said, tying up her papers, "that there's right words. That the same asking, in my words, is nothing, and in yours it's something. My trouble didn't change on the paper."

Marek cleaned the pen and said nothing, because she had just said, in one breath, at his own table, the truest thing he knew about his trade, and there were no better words for him to put it in. There. Even between friends: the instinct to improve a sentence that could not be improved.

Company paper spoke its own language back, and Marek was its reader-aloud as well as its writer-to.

When a notice went up on the board by the bank — and they went up in the dark of the morning, always already posted when the first shift walked in, as if the board itself had grown them in the night — the men who could not read stood before it in silence until Marek or another lettered man came by, and then the yard got its translation. Marek gave these readings in a flat voice, adding nothing, because the additions happened by themselves in every listening head.

It has been found necessary to adjust the rates paid upon second-quality coal.

Found necessary. By whom, found? Necessary to whom? The notice never said. Nothing in the Company's grammar ever said. Rates adjusted themselves, allocations were found insufficient, work was discontinued, men were turned off — the sentences arrived pre-cleaned of every hand that had done the thing, like tubs come up already weighed. There was no man anywhere in the Company's prose. There was only weather. And you could not argue with weather; you could only dress for it.

Marek noticed — had noticed for years, filed under the things a clerk notices and keeps in the drawer of his chest — that the grammar ran one direction only. When the Vesta broke a record month, the notice found its people fast enough: through the vigor of the management and the loyalty of the men. Agents appeared for the credit and vanished for the cost, regular as the cage. Grammar tracked blame the way water tracked slope, downhill and away from the office, every time, without a single exception in eleven years of notices, which was the kind of consistency that no honest weather ever managed.

He walked home the long way that evening, up past the chapel, and found Tomas on the step of the schoolroom with a slate, doing his letters in the last of the light, and the sight stopped him for a moment in the road.

The boy was making the words Marek had set him. Coal. Cage. Wage. Home. His hand was coming on well. Better than well — the boy had the clerk's neatness in him already, the trick of the even line.

Letters were the door. Marek believed that; his whole life was the proof of it, the screens to the cabin in one generation, and yet watching his son shape wage in a careful hand he felt the belief flex under him like a plank. Because he had the letters, all of them, the whole armory — the office phrases, the humble forms, the flat voice for the board — and what had they made him? The row's translator. The beam's mouth. A man who spent Sundays folding his neighbors' anger into shapes an office would consent to file. His letters had lifted him exactly as far as the Company had a use for a lifted man, and not one inch past it, and the moor road was as far from his door as from any hewer's.

Still he sat down on the step and corrected the slate, because wage wanted its silent letter, and the boy would need every silent letter he could get.

"Why's it spelled so, if it doesn't sound?" Tomas asked.

"Because the people who made the spelling don't ask us," Marek said, and the boy laughed, taking it for a joke.

Let him take it for a joke a while longer.

They walked down Prosper Row in the dusk, the boy's slate under the man's arm, past the doors of neighbors whose letters Marek had written and whose numbers Marek knew, and here and there a woman at a door bid them good evening, briefly, carefully, in the valley's own tongue —

which is to say: with the best words she could afford.
