Chapter Five — The Effort

The tin had a history before it had a purpose.

It had come into the house as a tea tin, in the good year — there had been one good year, early in the marriage, when the rates were up and Josef still worked the bank and the house had bought tea in a painted tin like an office family. The tea was long gone. The tin lived under the third floorboard from the window, and into it, for six years now, had gone whatever the week did not devour: a copper here, a threepence in a fat month, the odd shilling from Marek's evening copying. Lena called it the tin. Neither of them had ever once, in six years, said aloud what it was for, as if naming the sum would frighten it.

It had been, in the beginning, the Weld fund — three months' rent in advance, the toll of the moor road, the price of the unlocked door. Marek had reckoned that toll once at nine pounds, all told, moving a household. In six years the tin had never held two.

Somewhere in those years, without a word spoken, its purpose had quietly changed owners. It was Tomas's tin now. The lawyer's clerk in the market town took an apprentice at fourteen upon a premium of six pounds, and Tomas's hand was the best the chapel school had turned out in a decade — the schoolmaster said so, cautiously, the way men in the valley praised anything, as if praise drew weather — and six pounds by the boy's fourteenth birthday was four years off, and four years was fifteen shillings a year, and fifteen shillings a year was possible. Barely, and only if nothing happened.

That was always the clause. If nothing happened. Marek wrote it under every plan the way the office wrote upon the usual terms, and the difference between his clause and theirs was that theirs was enforceable.

To feed the tin, the household ran every hour it had.

Marek copied in the evenings — deeds and letters for the market-town lawyer, fair-copy work that came out by the carrier on Tuesdays and went back by him on Saturdays — clerk's work atop clerk's work, done by tallow after the yard's books closed, until his eyes gave out or the candle did, whichever came first; he had become a connoisseur of both kinds of failing light. Lena mended for three villages and had begun, this winter, taking in washing besides, which meant water carried, and water heated, and her hands at the year's end were a thing she kept out of the lamplight at supper. Col, across the row, was doing double turns in the low seam, putting for two crews, fifteen years old and asleep some nights at the table with the spoon in his hand, halfway through eating, like a soldier asleep on the march.

The chapel had a word for all this. The word was industry, and on the fourth Sunday of that winter the new minister, Mr. Corven, preached upon it, from Proverbs: the hand of the diligent maketh rich.

Corven was young, and gently born, and not a fool, and Marek rather liked him — he had got the screens boys a dinner-shed out of Mr. Fenn in his first year, which was one dinner-shed more than his predecessor had got in twenty. But he preached that morning to a gallery of hewers who had been underground before dawn six days of the week just ended, to women whose knuckles were split with other people's washing, to Col asleep upright in the third pew with his mother's shoulder for a pillow — he preached diligence, to that room, as the remedy for want, and Marek sat in his own pew doing the thing he could not stop doing, which was audit.

The hand of the diligent. He looked along the pews at the hands. There was not an idle hand in the chapel — there was not an idle hand in the valley; idleness was a luxury stocked, like tea in painted tins, elsewhere. Whatever ailed Prosper Row, it was not a shortage of the remedy Corven was prescribing. The row was drowning in the remedy. Every soul in it was at full stretch already, had been at full stretch so long that full stretch had stopped registering as effort and become simply the shape of a person, and if diligence made rich, the Vesta's rows should have shamed the city.

And yet — this was the part Marek turned over, walking home — the sermon worked. He watched it work. Men came out of the chapel straighter. Dess came out straighter. The sermon said: what happens to you is a wage, and wages can be earned, and the earning is in your own two hands. That was a terrible thing to say to people whose two hands were already spent — and it was also, Marek saw, the only comfortable thing anyone could say to them, because the other thing, the true thing, was: your hands are not the variable; the variable is held elsewhere, in rooms you will not enter, and no quantity of your sweat prices it. No one could walk out of chapel straighter on that. Corven preached the falsehood that stood men upright and not the truth that would have felled them, and Marek could not entirely blame him, and noted, in the sealed room, that the falsehood also cost the Company nothing and asked the Company nothing, and that of all the doctrines that could have blown into the valley and taken root, the one that flourished was the one that sent every man home to audit himself.

Wages could have been raised. Rents could have been lowered. The rates on the low seam could have been restored with one line in one letter. Each of those cost the Company coin. Diligence cost a sentence, and the sentence could be preached at any scale, to a boy or a pit or a whole grey valley, and its failure could never once be traced to the preacher — for however much a man had given, more had been possible. There was no floor of exertion beneath which the doctrine could be caught false, and a doctrine no fact can catch is not a doctrine about facts. It is a roof, built to keep something dry, and Marek knew what it kept dry, because he kept the books where that something was tallied.

From the cabin window, that winter, Marek watched the two kinds of effort go past on the same legs, and no one but he kept the distinction, because the distinction was a bookkeeping one, and he was the bookkeeper.

There was effort that built, and effort that merely held. Tomas's slate was the first kind: every evening's letters left a residue in the boy that made the next evening worth more — the hand surer, the figuring quicker, the premium four years off but the boy compounding toward it like interest. Marek's own copying was the first kind once removed: coin into the tin, the tin toward the premium. That effort had a floor under it and a ladder out of it.

And then there was the other kind, which was nearly all the kind the valley had. Col's double turns held his mother's account level — level, not falling — while his shilling in the pound quietly climbed. Lena's washing held the winter's coal. Aldany's crew, hewing the worst seam at the worst rate, held their own rents, which held the Company's dividend, and every stroke of it was consumed in the holding, ash by morning, nothing left in the arm or the account but the right to do it again Monday. Two men could spend the identical strength on the identical day — Marek watched them do it, daily, from his window — and one was laying a course of brick on a wall that would someday be a house, and the other was bailing a boat that leaked exactly as fast as one man could bail. From the bank, from the chapel, from the office, from any distance at all, the two looked the same. They looked like swimming.

The seam you bailed in was assigned before your first stroke. That was the entry the doctrine of diligence left off its page. Effort had an address, and the address had a landlord.

What happened, happened in February, because February was when things happened in the valley; even calamity kept the Company's calendar.

Josef took his bad turn on a Tuesday night — the cough's quiet coming, this once, in the wrong place, going long, and longer, until the house was up with candles at three in the morning and Tomas was running, nine years old in the black frost, for the doctor at the far end of the town.

The doctor came. The doctor was the Company's doctor — there was no other within seven miles; nearness, again, the dearest good in the valley — and he was competent, and kind enough, and he came twice that week and once the week after, and left physic from the apothecary's each time, and his account came to the house at the month's end with the Company's own punctuality. There was no question of not paying it. There was no question, either, of asking the Field Club, whose rules Marek could recite now like a catechism learned from the inside of a whip.

Marek took up the third floorboard on a Sunday in March, in front of Lena, because they had never once done anything with the tin except in front of each other, and counted out the doctor and the apothecary, and what went back under the board would have shamed the good year's tea.

One pound, four shillings. Six years.

Josef mended — mended to his new mark, each bad turn resetting the mark lower, the way each spring the sums for the moor road had come out a little worse — and that was the good side of the ledger and Marek held to it. The man was alive, and sat by the fire in April sun, and taught Tomas draughts, and the house was not in debt to anyone but the ordinary Thursday debt at the store. It could have gone far otherwise. In the row's terms this was a winter got through cheap.

But he found Lena that Sunday night at the cold hearth long after the house slept, sitting with her hands in her lap in the dark, and when he sat by her she said the thing the sermon had taught her to say, the thing millions of hearths heard in the dark in that age and every age after, the private inquest that opens by itself and calls only one defendant:

"What did we do wrong, Marek? Tell me where we went wrong with it, and I'll mend it. I've gone over every week of it and I can't find the week."

She could not find the week because the week did not exist. There was no week in which they had chosen ill. He could have laid the whole account before her true: the tin had been robbed by the rent held before touching, by the store's fifth, by the sixpences taken for a Club that would never pay them, by a doctor whose distance was priced like his physic, by rates found necessary in rooms eleven weeks and a hundred miles away — the tin had been robbed on a schedule, by hands that would never once appear in any week of hers, and her diligence and his had done everything diligence could do, which was to make the robbery slower.

He said some of that, the parts that could be said at a cold hearth at midnight without taking the roof off the world. He was not sure she believed him. The sermon was in her bones as it was in the whole valley's; it hurt less than his ledger did; his ledger said you were never the variable, and there is no comfort in that sentence for a woman audited by her own heart at her own hearth, none, though every word of it was true. She went up to bed comforted the crooked way, by the doctrine — resolved to try the harder, to find the week that wasn't there, next year, and find it sooner.

And the system received her resolution, as it received all the valley's resolutions, silently, the way the beam received tubs.

Marek sat on alone a while by the dead fire.

Stopping was not thinkable. He was clear-eyed about why: not hope. Testimony. To stop trying was to sign the doctrine's verdict — insufficient diligence, the fault found in the defendant as charged — and no man hands the court its confession. So the house would run every hour it had, next year as this year; the tin would fill by coppers and be robbed by schedule; and he would keep on writing the fairest hand in the valley in books that recorded everything except what was being done, and to whom, and by no one.

The effort would continue. All of it. Everyone's.

And the Company, receiving that effort constant regardless of what it paid for it — receiving more of it, indeed, the less it paid, for the emptier the tin the harder the bailing — the Company had learned the one lesson it was built to learn, the lesson its whole grey machinery ran on, from the beam to the board to rule the seventh:

that it did not need to change, and so it did not.
