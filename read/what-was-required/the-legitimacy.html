Chapter Four — The Legitimacy

The answer to Dess's letter came in eleven days, which was fast, and Marek knew what fast meant before she ever unwrapped the paper on his Sunday table.

Fast meant yes. The office answered its yeses briskly, because a yes cost coin and coin was simple. It was the noes that took months, because a no had to be dressed.

The Company is pleased to allow, the letter said, that one half of the wages of Col Dessin, putter, be received against the account of the late Havel Dessin — and Dess put her hand flat on the table and breathed once, deep, and Marek let her have the breath before he read her the rest, which was the sentence the first sentence had come to carry: the account to be extended accordingly, upon the usual terms, until discharged.

Upon the usual terms. The usual terms were a shilling in the pound, per annum, upon the balance outstanding. Marek did the sum in his head while Dess was still smiling, because his head did such sums without asking his leave: half the boy's wage against the debt, the debt growing by its shilling in the pound all the while — the payments would run nine years, and the Company would receive, in the end, half again what the original account had stood at, and Col would be a man of four-and-twenty when his father's dying was finally paid for.

He read her that part too. He was gentle with it, but he read it. He had made it a rule of the Sunday table years ago: the neighbors got the whole of a letter, not the half that was kind.

Dess listened, and worked it through, and then she said a thing Marek carried up to the cabin with him for the rest of his life.

"Still," she said, folding the letter away as if it were a good letter, because in the valley's arithmetic it was a good letter, "they heard me. That's something, Marek. They heard me."

They had. That was the product. Not the concession — the concession was priced to the farthing at what the office could grant without granting anything. The product was the hearing. Dess had gone to the wall with a grievance and come away with a correspondence; she was in the book now not only as a debtor but as a petitioner considered, and considered kindly, and something in her stood straighter for it, and would stand straighter at the store and the chapel and the screens. The Company had spent nothing and bought her very posture.

Marek understood, watching her tie the wrapping, that he was looking at the engine's smallest working model. It did not run on cruelty. Cruelty would have jammed it. It ran on the hearing — on the granted half, the extended term, the respectful reply — on grievance metabolized into gratitude for the metabolizing.

His father's business with the Field Club ran otherwise, because his father's business was a no, and the no arrived dressed, as noes did, in the finest cloth the office kept.

The Field Club was the pit's relief society. Every man of the Vesta paid into it, sixpence a week held from wages at the office — held the way rent was held, before any man touched his money — and out of the fund came the funeral payments, the smart-money for crushed hands and broken legs, the widows' small pensions. On the board by the bank, the Club's rules hung in a glazed frame, and rule the seventh said relief was payable to any member disabled from his work by accident occurring underground.

Josef could no longer cross a room in winter without stopping to lean. The dust had been fifteen years taking his wind — the dust of the Vesta's own seams, breathed in the Vesta's own dark, at the Vesta's own wage — and in the spring Marek had written his father's claim in the right words, every phrase oiled and fitted, and carried it to the Club's clerk himself.

The answer had taken four months, and it stood on two legs, and Marek had known both legs were coming and had written against them in advance, and it had mattered not at all.

First: the dust was not an accident. An accident, said the answer, citing the rules as scripture cites itself, was an event — a fall, a blast, a breaking — occurring at a time and place that could be sworn to. The dust had no time and place. It was fifteen years of every shift, which was to say it was nothing a man could point to, which was to say — in the grammar of rule the seventh — it had never happened. Josef was not disabled by an accident. He was merely dying of his employment, and there was no rule about that.

Second, and in the alternative, as the office loved to say: even were the dust ruled an accident, rule the eleventh required claims brought within twelve months of the injury sustained, and Josef's last shift underground lay nine years back, and so the claim, even if good, came late.

Nine years back, Josef had left the pit because his wind was failing. The wind was failing because of the injury. The injury could not be claimed then, because he could still work the bank; could not be claimed now, because the clock had run. The rule had built a door that was open only at a moment when no man alive would think to walk through it, and the Club's clerk — a decent man, Marek knew him, he sang bass in the chapel — had applied the rules exactly, and would have said, truly, that he had no discretion in the matter, and he would have been right. The calendar had performed the sentence. There was no one anywhere in the machinery of it for even Marek's careful anger to fix upon: only rules, applied; deadlines, expired; a fund, protected for the good of all members; and a man leaning on furniture to cross a room.

Josef himself took the answer without surprise. He had it read to him twice, nodded, and said only: "The sixpences, though. They'll keep holding the sixpences?"

They would. Membership was a condition of the bond. Josef's own sixpences, forty years of them, were in the fund the rules protected; his son's sixpences went in still, every week, held at the office, before touching. Josef laughed at that — his laugh now was three parts cough — and said the thing the office could never have understood was a worse verdict on it than any anger:

"Well. It's regular, any road. You know where you are with them."

You knew where you were with them. That was the terrible bedrock of it, Marek thought. Not love, not fear, not even habit exactly. Regularity. The rules were the same for the man they ground as for the man they spared, applied in the same order in the same words on the same schedule, and out of that sameness the valley made the thing the Company could never have bought from it directly, which was the sense that all this was not done to them but simply so — like winter, like the seam, like the price of candles.

Then came the summer of the petition, and Marek got his full schooling in what process did to heat.

It began with the notice on the board — found necessary to adjust the rates paid upon second-quality coal — which in the yard's plain sums meant a farthing off the hundredweight for every tub off the low seam, where the grey band ran, which meant the men hewing the worst coal in the wettest workings would now be paid least for it, and the yard's temper that week was a thing Marek could read from the cabin window in the set of shoulders at the cage.

The men met at the alehouse — not secretly; secrecy was for conspirators, and they were petitioners, they were exact about that — and on the Sunday a deputation of five came to Marek's table, caps in hand in his own house where he'd have had them covered, and asked him to draw their asking into right words. Aldany spoke for them. The asking had two parts: that the old rate be restored upon the low seam; and — here Aldany's voice did something careful — that the men be permitted, at their own cost, by their own subscription, to appoint a checkweighman to stand at the beam, as was formerly the custom.

The room held still around that sentence. Every man in it was thinking of Brant. Every man in it knew every other man was. No one said the name. The name would have made the asking an accusation, and they were petitioners.

And Marek — Marek sat with his pen over the paper, and inside the sealed room of his chest the screw sat snug in the beam not two hundred yards from his table, and he thought: I could put it in this letter. One sentence in his own clerk's hand: let the beam itself be tried against the stamped weights on a day the men appoint. That was all it would take. The sentence stood up in him, and walked to the front of his mind, and waited there with its hat in its hand while he wrote every other sentence of that petition, in the finest office dialect of his life — humbly sheweth — respectfully submit — as the Company may think fit — and when the letter was done and read back and marked by five hands, the sentence was still waiting, and he folded the paper and sealed it, and the sentence sat back down in the dark, and Marek told himself what he had told himself in the cabin: not yet, not this way, it would burn the petition it rode in on. A petition that accused was a petition refused. He was protecting the men's asking. That was what he told himself, and it had the advantage of being true, which the best lies to oneself always have.

What happened to the petition was nothing, administered beautifully.

It was received. Word came down in three days that it had been received — received graciously, the office's own messenger saying so at the bank, and the yard's shoulders eased a little at that, which Marek noted. Received was not granted. But the men had braced for the other thing, the cold thing, and graciousness landed on the braced muscles like rest.

Then it was referred. The rates being a matter of the general schedule, the petition must go before the board's quarterly sitting, which stood eleven weeks off. Eleven weeks was not a refusal. Eleven weeks was a date, and a date is a thing men can live toward, and the yard lived toward it, and the temper that had been a bank of coals in June was, by the quarterly sitting, a thing men had to remind one another they were feeling.

At the sitting it was considered. Consideration produced a request — the board, wishing to weigh the matter fully, required returns of the low seam's yields for the three years past, which returns must be compiled, and Marek himself compiled them, three weeks of evenings, fair copies in his best hand, and there was a bitter comedy in that which he did not share with Lena: the petition against the rates could not move without figures, and the figures came off the very beam the second part of the petition had asked leave to distrust, and nobody anywhere in the process saw the joke, or the joke was the process and no one was meant to see it.

In November the answer came, and it was a masterwork, and Marek read it aloud at the bank to the assembled shift in his flat translating voice while the sleet came sideways off the moor.

The board had given the matter its fullest consideration. The adjustment of the rates, having been found necessary upon grounds previously stated, must stand; the board noted, however, the men's evident and creditable concern for fair dealing, and had therefore directed that the beam and weights at the Vesta be inspected and certified twice yearly hereafter, as heretofore, by the proper officer; in view of which provision, the appointment proposed in the second part of the memorial was found to be unnecessary.

Found unnecessary. The men had asked to check the scale; the answer was that the scale would continue to be checked by the same hands that had always checked it, on the same calendar the screw already knew; and this — this was the stroke Marek almost admired — this was recorded as a thing granted, a provision directed, in response to the men's creditable concern. The file, henceforward and forever, would show that the men of the Vesta had petitioned upon the weighing, and that the matter had been reviewed, and met, and closed. The beam was more legitimate now than before the petition. They had objected at it, and their objection, processed correctly, had come out the far end as its endorsement.

The yard took the reading in silence and went to the cage. There was nowhere else to take it. The process had eaten five months, and the heat of June with them, and it was sleeting, and the shift was called.

Aldany stood a moment by the cabin after the others had gone down. He was not a bitter man; it was why they'd chosen him.

"Well," he said at last, to the yard in general, "we did it all in the right order, any road. Can't say we didn't do it in the right order."

"No," Marek said. "None can say that."

Aldany nodded slowly, put his lamp to his cap, and went to the cage, and Marek climbed his two steps and opened the day's page, and it was only in resetting his pencil that he found he had pressed the last point flat against the sill without noticing.

The petition had failed. That was not the finding Marek entered against the summer in his own reckoning. The petition had worked — worked exactly, worked the way the beam worked — taken in heat at one end and issued, at the other, a certificate that all was in order. He understood the whole machine now, standing at his window watching the cage drop: it did not require their belief; it had never required their belief; it required only their motion, and it had their motion, because rent was regular and winter was regular and the rules were the same for everyone —

— and it would go on having their motion until the day it did not; and nothing about that day could be petitioned into being, or would be announced when it set out; and the office had no form for it, no rule the seventh, no schedule; and that, Marek thought, pressing a new point onto his pencil, was the one entry in all its books the Company could neither dress nor date.

Six hundred tubs came over the beam that day.

He wrote every one.
