Chapter Three — The Obedience

The rule was called laying out, and it worked like this.

A tub came up from the dark with four hundredweight of coal in it, hewed by a man lying on his side in a seam thirty inches high, a mile out under the moor. If the banksman's eye or the clerk's probe found stone in it past the allowed measure — a few fists of the grey band that ran through the lower seam and came away with the coal whether a man in the dark willed it or not — then the tub was laid out. Forfeited. Not docked by the weight of the stone: forfeited whole, all four hundredweight, struck from the hewer's count as if it had never come up. The coal itself did not vanish. It went over the screens with the rest and into the Company's wagons and was sold in the market town at the market price. Only the paying of it vanished.

Marek had laid out eleven tubs the week the story properly begins, and it was only Tuesday.

He did not make the rule. He could not remember a time before the rule. It was printed in the bond every man signed at hiring — signed or marked, and Marek witnessed the marks — and it had been printed there when Marek's father marked it, and the ink had the settled look of scripture. When a hewer stood at the beam with his jaw working while his tub was struck off, what Marek said, when he said anything, was: "It's the bond." And the man would take it, because it was true, and because the clerk saying it plainly was not the same man as the Company doing it, and everyone in the yard kept that distinction polished, Marek most of all. It was the distinction he shaved by.

No one had ever asked Marek whether he agreed with the laying-out rule.

He noticed that, sometimes, the way you notice a sound that has stopped. In eleven years, through four overseers and two viewers, through thousands of tubs struck from thousands of counts, not one man above him had ever once asked the man at the beam what he thought of the rule he executed. Agreement was not in his articles. Belief was not in anyone's articles. The Company had never in its existence asked a single soul in the valley to believe anything, and Marek had come to understand, slowly, in the cabin's long afternoons, that this was not an oversight. It was the design, and it was brilliant, in the way the beam itself was brilliant.

Because belief was dear. Belief had to be argued into a man, and argued fresh with every man, and it could sour, and you could not inspect it. But motion — motion was cheap, and motion wrote its own receipt. A tub weighed was a tub in the book; a book kept was a clerk at his post; and what the clerk carried in his chest while he kept it, the Company could not have priced and did not need. Marek's interior was entirely his own. He dissented in it all day long. He laid out a widow's son's tub and inside the sealed room of himself he pronounced the rule a filthy engine, and his hand wrote the figure fair, and the Company had the figure, and Marek had the pronouncement, and of the two of them only one was worth anything at week's end, and both parties to the arrangement knew which.

He had a name for that sealed room, privately. He called it his integrity. On the bad days he suspected the Company had a name for it too, in some ledger of its own, and that the name was throughput.

Wednesday, the stone came up in Col's tub.

The boy was fifteen and putting for Aldany's crew in the low seam, where the grey band was worst, and the tub came onto the beam with the stone sitting right at the top, not even hidden, the way stone sat when a lad was hurrying because his mother's debt ate his wage and hurry was the only coin he had to add to it. The banksman looked at the stone and looked at Marek.

The measure was the measure. It was past the measure.

"Lay her out," Marek said, and wrote the stroke, and it took him no longer than the writing of any other figure, because he had made his hand learn years ago to move at one speed no matter what his chest was doing, the way you steady a lamp in a draught.

Four hundredweight, struck. A day of the boy's hauling, gone into the Company's wagons for nothing. It would come out of Dess's kitchen by Friday — Marek could have written the sum in the margin: the smaller loaf, the fire banked earlier, the boots resoled next month instead of this. He did not write it in the margin. The book had columns for tare and gross and forfeit. The book had no column for what the figure did after it left the page. That was not an oversight either. A column like that would have made every stroke of the pencil weigh what it actually weighed, and no clerk could have lifted the pencil by noon.

Col said nothing at the beam. Fifteen, and he'd already learned yard grammar. He looked at the needle, and said, "Beam's honest, any road?" — light, like a joke, the way the yard said serious things.

"She reads what she reads," Marek said.

The boy went back to the cage. Marek sat with the sentence he had just said, which he had said a thousand times, and which had tasted different this time, for reasons the rest of this book is about.

Understand the chain, because the chain is the whole answer to the question the row never quite asked aloud, which was: who does this to us?

The measure of allowed stone was set by Mr. Fenn, the viewer — but Fenn had it from the Company's board in the city, who had it from a schedule drawn by men who had never seen the Vesta, calculated from the selling price of coal and the cost of screening, and the board answered to owners, and the owners were heirs and widows and a cathedral fund, scattered across the country, who received their dividend twice a year and knew the Vesta as a line in a letter. Ansel, the bank overseer, enforced the measure but could not vary it. The banksman spotted the stone but did not judge it. Marek judged it against a measure he did not set, wrote it in a book he did not own, under a bond he only witnessed.

At every station on the line, a man could say with perfect truth: not mine. The hewer only filled the tub. The putter only pushed it. The banksman only called it. The clerk only wrote it. The overseer only kept the yard to the bond. The viewer only kept the pit to the schedule. The board only kept the concern to its dividend, and the dividend was owed to a widow in the south who had never in her life touched coal except to put it on her fire, and who would have wept, truly wept, at the sight of a nine-year-old at the screens in January.

Assembled, the chain did a thing that was in nobody's hands. Disassembled, it produced no one to ask. Marek had gone up and down it in his head a hundred nights, looking for the link where the wrong lived, the one man who, removed, would remove the thing itself — and there was no such link. Each man's portion was small enough to carry. Each man's portion was small enough to justify at his own fireside in one sentence. The engine's genius was exactly there: it had divided a weight no conscience could lift into parcels each conscience could pocket, and the parcelling was so fine that the weight was nowhere at all now — nowhere except at the bottom, entire, on the people under it, who experienced as one single crushing fact what everyone above them handled only as fractions.

Harm at scale had no flavor at the point of contact. A stroke of pencil. A measure applied. Rain, one drop of it, that somewhere out of sight was a flood.

There had been a man once who inspected the chain. The yard still had him to point to, which the yard understood, without saying so, to be one of his uses.

His name was Brant. Twenty years back — Marek had been a screens boy; he remembered the man's voice more than his face — the hewers of the Vesta had done a thing pits did in those days: chosen one of their own, by subscription, a farthing a man a week, to stand at the scale beside the Company's clerk and check the weighing on the men's behalf. Brant had letters, had figures, had a level eye, and the men trusted him, and for two years he stood at the beam and kept the men's own count in the men's own book, and twice in those two years he caught the tare set wrong and made the Company true it, and the Company thanked him with a courtesy that should have been a warning, because the Company was never courteous to anything it intended to let live.

Then the east headings went slack, and there were turnings-off, and Brant's crew was turned off entire — nothing personal, the trade being what it was — and when the headings took men on again in the spring, somehow Brant was not among the men taken, not that spring nor any spring after, at the Vesta nor at any pit in the valley nor the next valley, and no notice ever said why, because no notice ever said anything. He had not been punished. Punishment would have named the offense. He had simply become, everywhere at once and by no one's decision, a man there was no work for.

He was still about. He carted ashes and night-soil, mended fence for farmers, took what the seasons dropped. He was pointed out to new men, not often, and never as a warning exactly — as a curiosity, the way you'd point out a tree the lightning had picked once out of a whole wood. That's Brant, that stood checkweigh at the Vesta in the old days. And the new man would look, and file it, and the filing was the sentence. The men's scale-money subscription had never been raised again. When it came up, every few years, in the wet warmth of the alehouse, older men found the tobacco in their pipes suddenly needing great attention, and the subject drowned quietly, and everyone went home believing themselves to have decided nothing.

Marek, who kept accounts by nature, had once tried to reckon whether Brant had been right to do it. The two true-ings of the tare, over two years, against the whole rest of the man's life. It could not be made to balance. And then, because he was honest at least in the sealed room, he had turned the sum around: what the two years had been worth to the men — and that column would not close either, because there was no column for what it was worth to four hundred men to watch one of their own stand at the beam unafraid, and no column for what it cost them to watch what was done to him after, and to learn the price list, and to go home and teach it, wordlessly, to their sons.

Being right late is indistinguishable from being wrong, Marek had written in the almanac flyleaf, under the other sentence. Being right early is worse.

The thing with the beam happened on the Friday of that same week, and it happened because of a sack of onions.

Lena had traded mending for onions with a market-town carrier, and the carrier's sack was short, and Lena — who trusted no scale she had not seen born — had made Marek weigh it Friday evening on the cabin beam after the yard emptied, against the stamped half-hundred check-weight that lived in the cabin's locked chest, the Company's own reference, brought out twice a year when the inspector rode through.

The onions were honest. The beam was not.

He saw it in the settling of the needle, and at first he did not believe his eye, and then he was a clerk again and disbelief was not in his articles. He weighed the check-weight itself. He weighed it three times. The beam read it light — light by a whisker, a nothing, a shaving off the true — and a shaving is a nothing on one tub, and the Vesta swung six hundred tubs a day across that beam, and Marek stood alone in his own cabin in the lamplight doing, against every instinct of self-preservation he owned, the multiplication.

He checked the tare pins. He knew every part of that machine the way Lena knew her needles. The adjustment was not wear. Wear drifts soft and random. This was seated, snug, deliberate as a set screw, because it was a set screw — turned to where someone had turned it, and locked, and the lock varnished over, and the varnish old. Years old. It predated Marek's articles. It predated, he reckoned, staring at it with the lamp close, the last three inspections, all of which had passed the beam true, because the inspector tested it with the Company's check-weight on a day the Company appointed, and Marek let that thought arrive all the way and stand in the room with him: on those days it must have read true. Which meant it could be set true when wanted. Which meant somebody wanted, and unwanted, and knew the calendar.

He put the check-weight away and locked the chest. He put the onions in the sack. He stood in the dark cabin a long time.

Here is everything that stood between Marek and the saying of it, laid out like a tub's count, since he was afterward to lay it out for himself so many nights:

He had not set the screw. Someone above him had set it, or caused it set, or knowingly let it sit — someone with authority over beams, and over inspections, and over clerks. The inspector had passed it, and the inspector was the Crown's man, or the county's, and if the Crown's man was satisfied, what was a clerk's eye against the Crown's? Perhaps it was old error, varnished by an ignorant hand, meaning nothing. Perhaps it was known and allowed for, high up, in some adjustment of the rates he had no sight of — perhaps the schedule already assumed it, and truing the beam would only make the Company true the rates, and the men no richer, and one clerk poorer, permanently, in the particular way Brant was poorer. He had a father whose lungs were coal. He had a wife who took in mending. He had a son whose way out of the screens was a clerk's articles someday, articles that were given and taken by the very hands that knew the calendar. The road east stood open and cost more than he had. And the figure itself was a shaving. A whisker. Six hundred tubs a day of whisker, forever, off men paid by the hundredweight — no. Hold to the shaving. A shaving was survivable. A shaving was, on any single tub, on any single day, for any single man, almost nothing.

Almost nothing, times the Vesta, times the years. He was the one man in the valley placed to do that multiplication, and the one man in the valley whose place depended on never doing it aloud.

Marek blew out the lamp. He carried the onions home through the dark of Prosper Row, past the lit cracks of the doors of men whose tubs crossed his beam, and Lena met him at their own door and asked was the carrier's sack short, and Marek said —

"The sack was fair."

Which was true.

He hung up his coat. He ate his supper. He heard his father cough through the wall, wet, then dry, then quiet, and listened for the quiet to end, and it ended, and the night was ordinary.

Obedience did not feel like submission. It felt like keeping pace. It felt like a man doing his articles, which no one could fault, in a yard where procedures were followed — and procedures were followed was a true sentence, the truest in the valley, and Marek was eleven years in understanding that it was not a defense but a description, and one more week from understanding that it was the charge.

He slept. The beam slept. The screw held its setting in the dark, snug as scripture.

Six hundred tubs on Monday.
