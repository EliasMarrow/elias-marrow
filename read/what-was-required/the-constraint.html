Chapter One — The Constraint

The candle had learned his hand.

Marek could find it in the dark without groping — the stub on the shelf above the bed, the flint beside it — and he could light it in one strike on a good morning, which mattered, because candles were sold at the Company store and the Company store set its own prices, and a man who wasted strikes wasted tallow, and tallow was wages. He had done the sum so many times it no longer felt like a sum. It felt like weather.

Half past four, or near it. There was no clock in the house. There was the knocker-up, old Petra with her long pole, working her way down the row tapping windows for a copper a week, but Marek always woke before her tap. He woke the way people wake in houses where sound has consequences: his father two rooms of thin board away, coughing his slow black cough into a rag, needing whatever sleep the night still owed him. The cough had a rhythm Marek knew better than any hymn. Wet, then dry, then quiet, and it was the quiet you listened for. As long as the quiet ended, the night was ordinary.

It ended. Josef coughed again, faint through the wall, and Marek let his breath out and dressed in the dark to save the candle after all.

Lena was already up. She was always already up. He found her by the banked fire, coaxing it back with a splint, and the light came up orange along her forearms and showed him the mending on the table — other people's shirts, other people's coppers — that she had worked at until the tallow gave out the night before.

"There's bread," she said. "Don't wake Tomas yet. He has an hour."

"He should eat something warm."

"He should sleep warm. He can eat walking." She said it without looking up, and Marek didn't argue, because she was right, and because the argument they were not having was not about breakfast. Tomas was nine. In an hour he would walk to the pit bank with the other boys and stand at the sorting screens until dark, picking stone out of coal with his hands, for fourpence, and every warm thing they could put into an hour of his sleep was a thing the day could not take back out of him.

Marek had stood at those screens himself, at that age, in that same cold. He remembered what the cold did to fingers, and what the belts did to boys who got slow. It was not a memory he consulted. It lived in him the way the damp lived in the walls of the row — not thought about, simply structural.

He ate standing. Bread, and a scrape of dripping, and water, because tea was at the store and the store had had its week's money already. Then he took his coat off the nail and went out into the dark, into the frost, into the long black street of identical doors that the Company had built and the Company owned and the Company let to its men at a rent the Company held back from wages before any man ever touched them.

The row was called Prosper Row. Nobody laughed at that anymore. The joke had worn through years ago, the way a stair wears, from everyone stepping on it in the same place.

The walk to the Vesta pit was three-quarters of a mile downhill, and Marek did it in company with half the row, boots on frozen mud, lamps not yet lit because the sky was doing the last of that work for free. Nobody spoke much. Speech cost warmth. At the bottom of the hill the headgear of the Vesta stood black against the grey, the great wheel just beginning to turn as the first cage of the day went down, and the sound of it reached them the way it always did — not loud, but total, a sound that lived under the town like a floor lives under a house.

Dess fell in beside him at the lane's end, shawl over her head, her boy Col ahead with the other putters, fifteen and walking like the men because he was paid like a boy and worked like a man, and the walk was where he made up the difference.

"Cold," Dess said.

"Cold," Marek agreed.

That was the whole of it, and it was not nothing. It was what they could afford, said in full.

Dess had been a hewer's wife, and was now a hewer's widow, which in the Company's books was a different kind of account entirely. The Company did not put widows out of its houses. It converted them. Rent became debt, debt became service, and Dess worked the screens alongside the boys, a grown woman sorting stone with children, paying down a number that a clerk had written in a ledger the week her husband stopped being alive and started being a settled matter. Marek knew the number. He was a clerk. He knew everyone's numbers.

Knowing the numbers was his trade, and it had bought him everything he had — the fourth room, the boots without splits, the son at the screens instead of down the pit at nine — and there were mornings, walking in the dark beside people who had none of those things, when what he mostly felt about his good fortune was the size of the drop beneath it.

There had been a plan, once. There was still a plan. Marek kept it the way Josef kept his old hewer's lamp, polished, on the shelf, unlit.

Two valleys east, over the moor road, the ironworks at Weld paid its weighmen almost half again what the Vesta paid, and paid it in coin — real coin, spendable anywhere, not the Company's tokens that were worth a full value at the Company's store and a mocking value everywhere else. Marek had walked to Weld once, on a rare idle Sunday, to see it with his own eyes. It was real. The wage was real. He had asked, carefully, and a foreman had looked at his hands and his letters and said, plainly, that a weigh-clerk who could read and figure would find work there inside a fortnight.

Inside a fortnight. He had walked home doing sums, and the sums had been doing him ever since.

Because the cottage on Prosper Row went with the Vesta, and the day Marek gave his notice was the day the cottage's clock started. Out within the month. And the ironworks had wages but no houses, and the town around it had houses but wanted three months' rent held in advance from strangers, and three months' rent was more coin than Marek had ever held at one time in his life, because his wages came as rent already taken, store credit already spent, tokens already discounted. A man could not save his way to the moor road out of money that was designed to come back to its printer.

And Josef could not walk two valleys. Josef could not, on the bad weeks, walk to the chapel at the top of the row. The dust was patient. It took a man's wind the way the Company took rent — before he ever touched it, off the top, a deduction he never saw and could not refuse.

So the plan stayed on the shelf, polished, unlit. And each spring Marek took it down and did the sums again, and each spring the sums came out the same, and he put it back. He did not call this deciding. He was an honest clerk. He called it arithmetic.

The road out of the valley was open. Nobody guarded it. Men pointed to it sometimes, in the way men said nobody's chained to the Vesta, and this was true. The door stood unlocked. It was just far enough away that the walk to it cost more than a walking man had, and the cost was never written anywhere a man could point to in his turn. Marek had thought about this often, in the weigh-cabin, in the long afternoons: that the open road did a second job, quieter than its first. It turned every man who stayed into a man who had chosen. And a chosen life requires no remedy. The road did not need to be passable. It only needed to be pointable.

Visible and available are different words, he had written once, in the flyleaf of his almanac, in his small clerk's hand. He did not remember anymore what day had made him write it. There were too many candidates.

The store opened at six, and Marek stopped there on account of lamp oil, and stood in the queue among the women with their books.

Every family had a book at the store. Purchases on one page, wages against them on the other, and the two pages closed on each other at week's end like a mouth. The prices in the store ran a fifth above the prices in the market town — Marek had checked, once, methodically, pencil in the margin of a handbill, and then had folded the handbill into the fire, because the check had no purpose. The market town was seven miles. The store was here. A woman with six mouths and a book did not pay the store's fifth because she failed to know better. She paid it because Thursday's dinner could not wait for Saturday's walk. The store was not cheating anyone. It was simply selling the one thing it truly stocked, which was nearness, and nearness, to people whose time and legs were already sold, was the dearest good in the valley.

Poverty was expensive. Marek had understood this since before he had words for it, standing at the screens as a boy, watching his mother buy small because she could never afford the cheapness of buying large. The dear loaf by the slice. The coal by the pail, bought back at retail from the very seam it came out of. The boots that cost twice because they were bought half as good. There was no paradox in it. It was a pricing structure, and it worked, whether or not any single man had ever sat down and designed it, the way a millstone works on whatever is under it, out of pure shape.

He took his oil and had it written in the book, and the clerk behind the counter — a boy Marek had himself taught letters to, three winters ago, in the chapel schoolroom — wrote it fair and looked up and said, "Anything more, Master Marek?" with the little extra courtesy the whole row gave him, because he was the man at the scale, and every family's week crossed his beam.

"Nothing more," Marek said, and felt, as he always felt, the weight of the courtesy, which was the weight of being, himself, one of the things the row had to navigate.

At the pit bank the day was fully begun. The cage rattled in its guides. The screens started their shaking clatter, and the boys took their places along them, Tomas among them now, small in his father's cut-down coat, hands already going at the stone with the quick unthinking motion that Marek's own hands still remembered. The banksman called the tubs. The first corves came up with the first coal, and the whole black machinery of the morning engaged tooth by tooth, everything in its place, everything owned by someone who had never stood in this yard.

Marek climbed the two steps into the weigh-cabin, hung his coat, and opened the great ledger to the day's page, which was clean and ruled and waiting.

From the cabin's little window he could see most of what there was: headgear, screens, store, chapel, row upon row of Company roof running up the hill, and the moor road going east out of the valley, pale in the middle distance, empty as it was always empty.

He sharpened his pencil with the knife his father had given him, and set the point on the first line.

Nobody had ever ordered Marek to be at this desk. That was the thing he came back to, in the early quiet, before the first tub swung onto the beam. There was no chain, no whip, no sentence passed. There was rent, and the book at the store, and fourpence at the screens, and a cough behind a thin wall, and a road that anyone was free to take. Out of all that, every morning, a choice assembled itself and stood at his shoulder wearing his own coat, and every morning he made it, the way he was making it now, and it was his. That could not be denied. The last step was always his.

It was only that the last step was the whole of what they gave him, and the last step was never where anything was decided. The deciding had happened upstream, in the rents and the prices and the distances, in rooms he would never see, long before the choice was set walking down to meet him. The Company pointed at the last step and called it the whole journey. It was not lying, exactly.

It was bookkeeping.

The first tub came up to the beam. Marek licked his pencil.

What was required of him, at this hour of the morning, was only that he keep choosing.

He did.

There was nothing else on the table.
